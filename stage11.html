01

Why this stage exists

Stage 2 built the statistical testing machinery (t-tests, chi-square, hypothesis testing mechanics) that A/B testing is built on — but running the machinery correctly is only half of real experimentation competence. The other half is knowing the specific ways A/B tests silently go wrong in practice: an imbalanced randomization nobody noticed (SRM), a result that looked significant but was really just users reacting to novelty rather than genuine preference, a "significant" result that's actually one false positive out of twenty metrics tested, or a test stopped early because it "looked significant" on day 3. These failure modes are exactly what separates an interviewer's easy question ("what's a p-value") from the harder, more revealing one ("tell me about a time an A/B test result turned out to be misleading, and how you caught it") — and this stage builds the answer to both.

"Statistical Testing (Hypothesis Testing, A/B Testing, AUC/ROC)" appears as a named skill across your CVs — a genuine claim that deserves the real Level A/B/C treatment.

Level A — What I actually did
Applied hypothesis testing and statistical evaluation methods (t-tests, chi-square, and AUC/ROC for model evaluation, as covered in Stage 2/6) as part of the analytical work at Axtria — comparing groups, evaluating model performance, and drawing statistically grounded conclusions rather than eyeballing differences.
Level B — What I should understand deeply
The full mechanics of designing and running a proper A/B test end to end: power analysis and sample size planning, the specific failure modes (SRM, novelty effects, multiple testing, peeking), and how to interpret a result rigorously rather than just reading off a p-value. This is the natural, expected extension of the hypothesis-testing skill already on the CV, even where a specific large-scale product A/B test isn't a named CV claim.
Level C — Honest framing for anything beyond that
If asked about running a full-scale, high-traffic product experimentation platform (feature flags, automated stats pipelines, thousands of concurrent tests), the honest answer is: "My hands-on hypothesis-testing and statistical evaluation work was applied at the analysis level, not building experimentation infrastructure — I understand the platform-level concerns (SRM detection, sequential testing correction) conceptually, but haven't personally built that tooling." Never claim to have built or operated a large-scale experimentation platform that didn't happen.
03

Experiment Design Fundamentals

Before any statistics: what makes a test a fair, valid comparison in the first place.

Randomization, Control/Treatment & Causal Validity B

An A/B test's entire causal validity rests on proper randomization: every unit (user, session, account) has an equal, independent chance of landing in control or treatment, so any systematic difference in outcomes can be attributed to the treatment itself rather than pre-existing differences between groups. This is what elevates an A/B test above the correlation-only observational analysis from Stage 2 — controlled randomization is specifically what allows a causal claim, not just a correlational one.

Worked example
import numpy as np, pandas as pd # Randomly assign 10,000 users to control/treatment via hashed user_id users["bucket"] = users["user_id"].apply( lambda uid: "treatment" if hash(str(uid) + "exp_v1") % 2 == 0 else "control" ) print(users["bucket"].value_counts()) # control: 5,012 treatment: 4,988 -- close to a clean 50/50 split # Verify pre-experiment covariates are balanced (a "balance check") print(users.groupby("bucket")["avg_past_purchases"].mean()) # control: 3.41 treatment: 3.39 -- no meaningful pre-existing difference
Hashing on user_id gives a stable, reproducible 50/50 split, and the balance check confirms the two groups looked statistically identical before the treatment was ever applied — this pre-experiment balance check is exactly what justifies attributing any later outcome difference to the treatment itself, not to a lucky or unlucky group composition.
Daily-lifeA fair coin flip deciding which of two identical-looking routes you take to work each day — no reason to expect one route's users to be systematically different people.
ConsumerEvery major tech company's "you're seeing a new feature" rollout is typically a randomized A/B test behind the scenes, not a blanket launch.
BusinessRandomly assigning half of a call list to a new sales script versus the old one, so any difference in conversion is attributable to the script, not to which reps happened to get which customers.
EnterpriseDirectly relevant to Axtria-style work — a genuine hypothesis-testing/A/B comparison of two outreach approaches would need exactly this kind of clean randomization to support a causal "this approach worked better" conclusion.
Interview"Why can't you just compare users who happened to opt into a feature versus those who didn't?" — self-selection means the two groups likely differ in ways that also affect the outcome (more engaged users are more likely to opt in AND more likely to convert), confounding the comparison — exactly Stage 2's correlation-vs-causation trap, applied to experimentation specifically.
Your CV"I'd describe proper randomization as the non-negotiable foundation of any hypothesis test I'd stand behind — without it, a 'significant' result doesn't actually support a causal claim."

Choosing the Right Metrics: Primary, Guardrail & Novelty B

A well-designed test defines its metrics before launch: a single primary metric (the one the decision hinges on, chosen to directly reflect the hypothesis), guardrail metrics (things that must not get meaningfully worse even if the primary metric improves — e.g. page load time, unsubscribe rate), and sometimes secondary/diagnostic metrics for understanding why a result happened. Choosing metrics after seeing the data (hunting for whichever metric looks significant) is a serious, common malpractice.

Worked example
# Pre-registered BEFORE launch, in the experiment design doc: # Primary metric: conversion_rate (call -> sale) # Guardrail metrics: avg_call_duration (shouldn't blow up), rep_satisfaction_score # Secondary metrics: time_to_first_response, follow_up_rate # Result: # conversion_rate: +2.1 pp (significant, p=0.008) -> primary metric moved # avg_call_duration: +45 sec (not significant, p=0.19) -> guardrail held # rep_satisfaction: -0.3 pts (significant, p=0.03) -> guardrail FAILED
Even though the primary metric improved significantly, one pre-registered guardrail (rep satisfaction) failed — a well-designed test catches this tradeoff explicitly rather than only reporting the flattering primary-metric win, which is exactly the discipline that separates rigorous experimentation from result-shopping.
Daily-lifeDeciding beforehand that a new commute route's success will be judged on total time, not "whichever measure happens to look better afterward" (distance, number of turns, scenery).
ConsumerA checkout redesign's primary metric might be conversion rate, with page-load-time and cart-abandonment as guardrails that must not silently get worse.
BusinessA new sales script's primary metric is conversion rate, but rep burnout/satisfaction is a real guardrail — a script that converts better but burns out the sales team isn't a clean win.
EnterpriseDirectly applicable to any hypothesis-testing work at Axtria comparing outreach strategies — a rigorous comparison needs a pre-defined primary metric, not a post-hoc scan of whichever number moved.
Interview"What's wrong with checking 15 different metrics and reporting whichever one is significant?" — that's p-hacking/multiple-testing malpractice (covered fully later in this stage) — with enough metrics checked, some will look significant by chance alone, even with no real effect.
Your CV"Pre-registering the primary metric and guardrails is exactly the discipline I'd point to if asked how I made sure a hypothesis-testing result was trustworthy, not just convenient."
04

Power, Sample Size & Minimum Detectable Effect (MDE)

Deciding how big a test needs to be before launching it — one of the most commonly skipped steps in real-world experimentation.

Statistical Power & Sample Size Calculation B

Statistical power is the probability of correctly detecting a true effect if one exists (1 - beta, the false negative rate) — conventionally targeted at 80% or 90%. Required sample size depends on four things: the baseline conversion rate, the minimum detectable effect (MDE) you want to be able to detect, your significance level (alpha, usually 0.05), and your desired power — smaller MDEs and higher power both require larger samples.

Worked example
from statsmodels.stats.power import NormalIndPower from statsmodels.stats.proportion import proportion_effectsize baseline_rate = 0.10 # current 10% conversion rate mde = 0.02 # want to detect a 2 percentage-point lift (to 12%) effect_size = proportion_effectsize(baseline_rate + mde, baseline_rate) analysis = NormalIndPower() n_per_group = analysis.solve_power(effect_size, power=0.8, alpha=0.05, ratio=1) print(round(n_per_group)) # 3,841 per group -- 7,682 total # If we only wanted to detect a larger 5pp lift instead: effect_size_5pp = proportion_effectsize(0.15, 0.10) n_5pp = analysis.solve_power(effect_size_5pp, power=0.8, alpha=0.05, ratio=1) print(round(n_5pp)) # 588 per group -- much smaller!
Detecting a smaller effect (2pp) requires roughly 6.5x more sample than detecting a larger one (5pp) — a concrete illustration of why "we want to detect even tiny improvements" is an expensive ask that trades off directly against how long a test needs to run, a tradeoff worth naming explicitly rather than picking a sample size arbitrarily.
Daily-lifeNeeding to taste many more spoonfuls of soup to detect "slightly less salty" than to detect "way too salty" — smaller differences need more samples to reliably notice.
ConsumerAn app deciding it needs 50,000 users in a test, not 500, because the expected lift from a subtle UI tweak is small.
BusinessCommitting upfront to running a test for 3 weeks (to reach the needed sample size) rather than "however long it takes to feel confident."
EnterpriseAny hypothesis-testing comparison at Axtria between two outreach approaches would need this exact pre-launch sample-size calculation to avoid running an underpowered test that can't reliably detect a real difference.
Interview"What happens if you run a test with too small a sample size?" — the test is underpowered, meaning even a real effect has a high chance of failing to reach statistical significance (a false negative) — you'd wrongly conclude 'no effect' when one actually exists.
Your CV"I'd walk through this exact power-analysis logic if asked how a test's duration or sample size was actually decided, rather than an arbitrary 'we ran it for two weeks.'"

Type I/II Errors Revisited & the Power-Sample Size-MDE Triangle B

Building directly on Stage 2's Type I (false positive, alpha) and Type II (false negative, beta) errors: power, sample size, MDE, and alpha are four interconnected knobs — fixing any three determines the fourth. In practice, teams usually fix alpha (0.05) and power (0.8), then solve for the sample size needed given a chosen MDE, or solve for the MDE detectable given a fixed sample size/timeline constraint.

Worked example
# Business constraint: only 2,000 users per group are realistically available in 1 week # Question: what's the smallest effect we COULD detect with that sample? from statsmodels.stats.power import NormalIndPower analysis = NormalIndPower() # Solve backwards for effect_size given fixed n detectable_effect = analysis.solve_power(nobs1=2000, power=0.8, alpha=0.05, ratio=1) print(round(detectable_effect, 4)) # 0.089 (Cohen's h) -- roughly a 4.5pp lift on a 10% baseline
Given the realistic sample-size constraint, the test can only reliably detect a lift of roughly 4.5 percentage points or more — if the true effect is smaller than that (very plausible for a minor UI tweak), the test is essentially guaranteed to come back "not significant" regardless of whether a real, smaller effect exists — a crucial thing to know and communicate before launching, not after a disappointing result.
Daily-lifeKnowing in advance that a short, casual taste test with 3 people can only reliably catch a huge difference in spiciness, not a subtle one — setting expectations before tasting, not after.
ConsumerA startup with limited traffic honestly acknowledging their test can only detect large product changes, not fine-tuning tweaks.
BusinessTelling a stakeholder upfront "given our traffic, we can only reliably detect a 5%+ improvement in two weeks" avoids a false "the change didn't work" conclusion when it may have had a real, smaller effect.
EnterpriseA genuinely useful pre-launch conversation to have proactively at Axtria — flagging when a planned comparison is underpowered given realistic data volume, before wasting a test cycle on it.
Interview"If a test comes back 'not significant,' does that prove there's no effect?" — no; it could mean there's genuinely no effect, OR the test was underpowered to detect the effect size that actually exists — 'absence of evidence isn't evidence of absence,' one of the most important nuances in experimentation.
Your CV"This exact distinction — 'not significant' versus 'genuinely no effect' — is something I'd flag proactively if presenting any hypothesis-testing result, to avoid a stakeholder over-interpreting a null result."

Sample Ratio Mismatch (SRM)

One of the single most important, most commonly-missed sanity checks in real-world experimentation.

Detecting SRM with a Chi-Square Goodness-of-Fit Test B

Sample Ratio Mismatch occurs when the actual observed split between control and treatment deviates meaningfully from the intended split (e.g. intended 50/50, actually observed 52/48) — a strong signal that something is broken in the randomization or logging pipeline, and that ANY result from the test should be distrusted until the cause is found and fixed. Detected using a chi-square goodness-of-fit test comparing observed group sizes to expected group sizes.

Worked example
from scipy.stats import chisquare observed = [10432, 9568] # control, treatment -- intended 50/50 on 20,000 users expected = [10000, 10000] chi2, p_value = chisquare(observed, f_exp=expected) print(chi2, p_value) # chi2=37.25, p-value=0.0000000010
A p-value this small (far below the standard 0.001 SRM-specific threshold, stricter than the usual 0.05) means this 4.3-percentage-point imbalance is essentially impossible to have occurred by chance under correct 50/50 randomization — this is a textbook SRM alert, and the correct response is to halt analysis and investigate the pipeline, not to proceed and report the primary metric result as if it were trustworthy.
Daily-lifeFlipping a coin 20,000 times and getting 10,432 heads versus 9,568 tails would be so unlikely for a fair coin that you'd immediately suspect the coin (or the flipping process) is broken, not that you got "unlucky."
ConsumerA famous real-world example: Bing's search-ranking A/B tests have publicly documented SRM caused by bot traffic disproportionately hitting one arm — a real, well-known failure mode, not a theoretical concern.
BusinessA test where the "new checkout flow" arm mysteriously has 8% fewer users than expected might mean the new flow is silently crashing or timing out for some users before they're even logged as assigned — a critical bug, not a real result.
EnterpriseA genuinely important sanity check for ANY randomized comparison at Axtria — if the "new outreach script" group ends up smaller than intended, that imbalance alone could bias every downstream metric before any real analysis even starts.
Interview"Why would you check for SRM before looking at the primary metric result at all?" — because a real SRM almost always means something is broken (logging, randomization, differential dropout) that will bias every other metric in the test — checking the headline result before ruling out SRM risks confidently reporting a completely invalid conclusion.
Your CV"SRM checking is the single sanity-check habit I'd most want to demonstrate — it's the kind of rigor that separates someone who's run hypothesis tests from someone who's run them carefully."

Novelty & Primacy Effects

A result that looks real on day 3 can evaporate — or appear — by week 3, purely because of how people react to change itself.

Novelty Effects, Primacy Effects & Trend-Over-Time Analysis B

A novelty effect is a temporary boost (or drop) in a metric purely because users are reacting to something being new/different, which fades as they adjust — inflating an early "win" that won't persist. A primacy effect is the opposite: engaged/loyal users are initially resistant to a change (their established habits are disrupted), understating a genuinely good change's true long-run effect until they adapt. Both are diagnosed by plotting the treatment effect over the course of the experiment, not just reading the final aggregate number.

Worked example
# Daily treatment effect (treatment - control conversion rate), by day of test daily_effect = { "day1": 0.041, "day2": 0.038, "day3": 0.035, # strong early lift "day7": 0.019, "day10": 0.012, "day14": 0.009 # decaying toward a smaller, more stable effect } # A 2-day test would have reported the primary metric as +3.8pp (misleadingly high) # The stabilized, trustworthy long-run effect is closer to +0.9pp
The effect clearly decays from +4.1pp on day 1 to +0.9pp by day 14 — if the test had been analyzed and shipped based on the first few days alone, the decision would have been made on a novelty-inflated number roughly 4x larger than the true stabilized effect, a very real, very common way experimentation goes wrong in practice.
Daily-lifeA new gym membership feeling exciting and motivating for the first two weeks (novelty), then usage settling back down to a more realistic, sustainable baseline — evaluating "does this gym plan work" only in week one would badly overstate it.
ConsumerA redesigned app icon or UI often gets an initial curiosity-driven engagement spike that has nothing to do with the redesign's actual long-term merit.
BusinessA new sales script might initially outperform simply because reps are more engaged/careful while it's new and being watched — the "Hawthorne effect," a close cousin of novelty bias.
EnterpriseA directly relevant caution for any Axtria-style comparison of a new outreach approach — an early strong result should be validated by running long enough to see whether the effect stabilizes, not shipped on a day-3 read.
Interview"How would you distinguish a real, durable treatment effect from a novelty effect?" — plot the daily/weekly treatment effect over the test's duration; a novelty effect shows a declining trend toward a smaller, stable value, while a genuine effect stays roughly flat throughout.
Your CV"I'd always want to look at the trend over time, not just the final pooled number, before trusting any 'this worked' conclusion from a hypothesis test."
07

The Multiple Testing Problem

Checking enough metrics (or enough test variants) practically guarantees a "significant" result purely by chance — the math behind why, and the standard fixes.

Family-Wise Error Rate, Bonferroni & FDR Correction B

Testing 20 independent metrics at alpha=0.05 each means the probability of at least one false positive (the family-wise error rate) is 1 - (0.95)^20 ≈ 64%, not 5% — a huge, often-overlooked inflation. Bonferroni correction (divide alpha by the number of tests) is simple but conservative, increasing false negatives. The Benjamini-Hochberg (FDR) procedure controls the expected proportion of false discoveries among significant results instead, offering more statistical power while still controlling error rate — usually the more practical default for many simultaneous comparisons.

Worked example
from statsmodels.stats.multitest import multipletests p_values = [0.001, 0.008, 0.015, 0.021, 0.043, 0.089, 0.15, 0.31, 0.52, 0.77] # Family-wise error rate with 10 metrics at alpha=0.05, uncorrected: fwer = 1 - (0.95 ** 10) print(round(fwer, 3)) # 0.401 -- a 40% chance of at least one false positive reject_bonf, p_bonf, _, _ = multipletests(p_values, alpha=0.05, method="bonferroni") reject_fdr, p_fdr, _, _ = multipletests(p_values, alpha=0.05, method="fdr_bh") print("Bonferroni significant:", sum(reject_bonf)) # 2 print("FDR significant:", sum(reject_fdr)) # 4
Testing 10 metrics uncorrected carries a 40% chance of at least one spurious "significant" result — Bonferroni's stricter correction leaves only 2 of the 10 metrics significant, while the less conservative FDR procedure retains 4, illustrating the real tradeoff: Bonferroni minimizes false positives at the cost of more false negatives, FDR balances both more evenly.
Daily-lifeFlipping 20 different coins once each and being unsurprised if one comes up "unusually" heads-heavy purely by chance — the more coins you check, the more likely you'll spot a fluke.
ConsumerA famous cautionary tale: checking dozens of astrological signs against an outcome will "find" a significant one purely by chance — the multiple-testing problem in its most vivid form.
BusinessRunning one A/B test but checking 25 different segment/metric breakdowns (by region, device, age group...) and reporting "it worked for mobile users in the Northeast" without correction is a classic, tempting, and statistically invalid move.
EnterpriseA real, practical concern for any Axtria-style hypothesis-testing work comparing outcomes across many territories or rep segments simultaneously — correction is needed before trusting any single segment's "significant" result.
Interview"Why is Bonferroni sometimes considered 'too conservative'?" — dividing alpha by a large number of tests makes it much harder for any individual real effect to reach significance, inflating the false negative rate — a real cost, which is why FDR-based methods are often preferred when many comparisons are genuinely expected to matter.
Your CV"This is exactly the caveat I'd raise if presenting a result from checking multiple metrics or segments — proactively, not only if asked."

Sequential Testing & the Peeking Problem

Why checking a live test's p-value every day and stopping as soon as it looks significant is one of the most common — and most damaging — experimentation mistakes.

Why "Peeking" Inflates the False Positive Rate B

A standard (fixed-horizon) significance test assumes you check the result exactly once, at a pre-determined sample size. Checking repeatedly and stopping the moment p < 0.05 ("peeking") dramatically inflates the true false positive rate — because you're effectively giving chance many separate opportunities to produce a fluke significant result, and stopping the instant it does. Proper fixes: commit to a fixed sample size/duration decided in advance (simplest), or use a formal sequential testing method (e.g. alpha-spending, always-valid p-values) explicitly designed to allow safe, repeated interim looks.

Worked example
# Simulation: true effect = 0 (no real difference), alpha = 0.05 # Simulate 10,000 experiments, checking significance every day for 20 days import numpy as np false_positive_count = 0 for sim in range(10000): stopped_early = False for day in range(1, 21): p = simulate_daily_pvalue_under_null() # true null, p ~ Uniform(0,1) each look if p < 0.05: false_positive_count += 1 stopped_early = True break print(false_positive_count / 10000) # ~0.64 -- a 64% false positive rate from 20 days of peeking, vs. the intended 5%
Even though there's genuinely no real effect in this simulation, checking daily and stopping at the first p<0.05 produces a false "significant" result roughly 64% of the time — nearly 13x the intended 5% error rate — a stark, concrete demonstration of exactly why peeking is one of the most damaging common mistakes in real-world experimentation, not just a theoretical nuance.
Daily-lifeFlipping a coin repeatedly and declaring "it's rigged!" the very first moment you happen to see 3 heads in a row — of course that will happen eventually if you keep looking, even with a perfectly fair coin.
ConsumerA team excitedly shipping a change because "the dashboard showed significance this morning!" without having pre-committed to that as the stopping point is a textbook peeking mistake.
BusinessStopping a promising-looking pilot program after just a few good days, rather than the originally planned month, risks shipping a decision based on noise rather than a real, stable effect.
EnterpriseA genuinely important discipline for any Axtria-style test — committing to a pre-determined test duration/sample size upfront, and resisting the pressure to "call it early" the moment a result looks favorable.
Interview"What would you say if a stakeholder wants to stop a test early because it 'already looks significant'?" — explain the peeking problem concretely (ideally with a number like the simulation above), and either hold to the pre-committed sample size or, if early stopping is genuinely needed, use a proper sequential-testing method designed for it rather than an ad-hoc early look.
Your CV"Explaining the peeking problem with a concrete simulated false-positive-rate number, like above, is exactly the kind of technically grounded pushback I'd give if pressured to call a test early."
09

Alternative Methods — Why This, Why Not That

A/B testing is one tool among several for measuring causal impact — knowing when each of the alternatives is the better fit.

Why a standard A/B test over a multi-armed bandit? A/B testing splits traffic evenly and analyzes once at the end, prioritizing a clean, statistically rigorous, easy-to-explain comparison; a multi-armed bandit dynamically shifts traffic toward the better-performing arm during the test, minimizing "regret" (lost conversions from serving the worse variant) — better when the cost of serving an inferior variant is high and a rigorous causal readout matters less than practical performance during the test itself. A/B testing is generally the better choice for confident, generalizable causal learning; bandits are better for pure short-term optimization.

Why a randomized A/B test over an observational/quasi-experimental causal inference method (e.g. difference-in-differences, propensity score matching)? True randomization is the gold standard for causal validity — it eliminates confounding by design. Observational causal methods are the fallback when randomization is genuinely impossible (e.g. you can't randomly assign which regions get a policy change) and try to approximate a causal estimate from non-randomized data, but always carry more assumptions and more room for hidden confounding than a real randomized test.

Why an A/B test over a simple before/after comparison? A before/after comparison confounds the treatment effect with anything else that changed over time (seasonality, external events, a concurrent change) — an A/B test's simultaneous control group isolates the treatment's effect from time-based confounds, which a before/after design fundamentally cannot do.

Why a user-level randomized test over a switchback (time-based) test? Switchback tests randomize by time period rather than by user (e.g. odd days get treatment, even days get control) — useful when user-level randomization isn't feasible (e.g. a marketplace-wide pricing change affecting all users at once), at the cost of needing to worry about carryover effects between periods that user-level randomization avoids.

Why not skip experimentation entirely and just ship based on intuition or a stakeholder's confidence? Because intuition and confidence are exactly what randomized testing exists to check against reality — the entire discipline built in this stage exists precisely because plausible-sounding changes frequently turn out to have no effect, or even a negative one, when actually measured rigorously.

Red Flags — Never Say This

"A p-value of 0.03 means there's a 97% chance the effect is real." A direct misinterpretation of what a p-value means (Stage 2's material) — it's the probability of seeing data this extreme (or more) if the null hypothesis were true, not the probability the alternative hypothesis is correct.
"We checked and it looked significant on day 2, so we shipped it." A textbook peeking mistake — exposes a lack of understanding of how dramatically repeated early looks inflate the false positive rate.
"SRM doesn't matter if the primary metric result still makes intuitive sense." SRM signals something is structurally broken in the experiment's data pipeline — a result that "makes sense" despite an SRM alert is not evidence the SRM doesn't matter, it's a coincidence that shouldn't be trusted.
"We tested 30 metrics and 2 came back significant, so those 2 are real wins." Without multiple-testing correction, roughly 1-2 significant results out of 30 tested is exactly what pure chance alone would predict — this is the multiple testing problem in action, not a real finding.
"An A/B test always tells you the true long-term effect." Novelty and primacy effects mean a short test's measured effect can differ meaningfully from the true long-run steady-state effect — always worth checking the trend over time, not just the final number.
