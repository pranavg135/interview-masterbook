Mathematics Foundations

Not a full math degree — the specific pieces that show up directly inside the models and tests in later stages. Each one exists here because something in Part IV–VI needs it.

Linear Algebra B

Vectors and matrices are how every model in Stage 6–8 actually represents data — a row of features is a vector; a whole dataset is a matrix. The dot product measures alignment between two vectors, which is the core operation inside linear regression and logistic regression's linear combination step.

y = w·x + b  =  w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + … + wₙxₙ + b

Why it matters later: PCA (Stage 6) is entirely eigenvectors/eigenvalues of the covariance matrix — you don't need to derive it by hand, but you need to know an eigenvector is "a direction the data doesn't rotate away from," which is exactly what PCA exploits.

Worked example
  1. A rep-scoring model: w = [2.5, −1.0, 0.8] for [calls/day, years experience, territory size], bias b = 10
  2. A rep's features: x = [3, 5, 40]
  3. y = w·x + b = (2.5×3) + (−1.0×5) + (0.8×40) + 10 = 7.5 − 5 + 32 + 10
y = 44.5 — the model's raw score for this rep, before any activation function is applied. This dot product is the entire computation inside one prediction of linear/logistic regression.
Daily-life analogyA weighted GPA calculation — each grade (feature) times its credit hours (weight), summed — is literally a dot product.
Consumer exampleA credit score blends several factors (payment history, utilization, age of credit) with different weights into one number.
Business exampleA lead-scoring model weighting company size, engagement, and industry into one priority score for sales.
Enterprise exampleRecommendation engines represent users and items as vectors and rank by dot-product similarity at massive scale.
Interview example"What does a regression coefficient actually represent geometrically?" — the weight on one axis of this dot product, i.e. how much that feature tilts the prediction.
Your CVThe Axtria efficiency-scoring model that fed the rep allocation decision is doing exactly this calculation per rep.

Calculus — Derivatives & Gradients B

A derivative tells you the slope — how much the output changes per tiny change in input. A gradient is just that idea extended to many inputs at once: the direction of steepest increase.

θ ← θ − α · ∇L(θ)

Why it matters later: that one line is gradient descent — literally how XGBoost, logistic regression, and every neural network in Stage 8 & 12 learn. "Walk me through how a model trains" almost always comes back to this.

Worked example
  1. Minimize L(θ) = (θ − 4)², learning rate α = 0.1, start at θ = 0
  2. Gradient: dL/dθ = 2(θ − 4). At θ=0: gradient = 2(0−4) = −8
  3. Update: θ ← 0 − 0.1×(−8) = 0.8
  4. Step 2 — gradient at θ=0.8: 2(0.8−4) = −6.4 → θ ← 0.8 − 0.1×(−6.4) = 1.44
  5. Step 3 — gradient at θ=1.44: 2(1.44−4) = −5.12 → θ ← 1.44 − 0.1×(−5.12) = 1.952
θ climbs 0 → 0.8 → 1.44 → 1.95 → … converging toward θ=4, the true minimum — this exact loop, thousands of times, is model training.
Daily-life analogyWalking downhill in fog by always stepping in the steepest downward direction you can feel underfoot — you'll reach the bottom without seeing the whole map.
Consumer exampleA thermostat's auto-adjust nudging temperature closer to your target setting a little at a time, not jumping straight there.
Business exampleIteratively refining a pricing model's parameters against last quarter's actual sales data until predictions stop improving.
Enterprise exampleEvery production ML model at scale — fraud detection, ranking, forecasting — is trained via some variant of this update rule.
Interview example"What happens if the learning rate is too high?" — the updates overshoot the minimum and can diverge instead of converge; too low, and training takes forever.
Your CVThe XGBoost and Logistic Regression models co-developed at Axtria were both trained via gradient-based optimization of this exact form.

Probability Rules A

Three rules carry almost everything: P(A and B) = P(A)·P(B|A) (multiplication rule), P(A or B) = P(A)+P(B)−P(A and B) (addition rule), and Bayes' theorem for reversing a conditional.

P(A|B) = P(B|A)·P(A) / P(B)
Worked example
  1. A disease affects 1% of a population: P(Disease) = 0.01
  2. A test correctly detects it 90% of the time: P(Positive | Disease) = 0.90
  3. The test false-positives 5% of the time on healthy people: P(Positive | No Disease) = 0.05
  4. P(Positive) = P(Positive|Disease)·P(Disease) + P(Positive|No Disease)·P(No Disease) = 0.90×0.01 + 0.05×0.99 = 0.009 + 0.0495 = 0.0585
  5. P(Disease | Positive) = 0.90 × 0.01 / 0.0585 = 0.009 / 0.0585
P(Disease | Positive) ≈ 15.4% — even after a positive test, most people testing positive don't have the disease, because the disease is rare. This is the exact intuition behind why a flagged transaction, flagged churner, or flagged fraud case is usually still more likely "not the thing" when the base rate is low.
Daily-life analogyA smoke alarm that's very sensitive still cries wolf constantly if false fires are rare — most alarms won't mean an actual fire.
Consumer exampleA spam filter flagging an email doesn't mean it's definitely spam if spam itself is rare in your inbox that week.
Business exampleA churn model outputs P(churn | flagged) = 40% — a completely different number from P(flagged | churn), and interviewers ask this specifically to catch the swap.
Enterprise exampleFraud-detection systems at scale (banks, payment processors) run exactly this base-rate math to decide alert thresholds — too sensitive and investigators drown in false positives.
Interview example"If P(defect|flagged)=90%, is 90% of flagged output defective?" — yes, that's literally what the conditional means, but only if you set up the conditioning correctly.
Your CVThe Axtria forecasting model's outputs (e.g. "likely to churn / high-priority rep") are exactly this kind of conditional probability — worth being able to state precisely what the model's output number means.

Optimization Basics C

An objective function to maximize or minimize, decision variables you control, and constraints that bound them. This exact vocabulary is what Stage 9 builds into the full LP formulation behind your Axtria allocation work.

max Σ (revenue per rep)  s.t.  Σ reps ≤ 60, cost-per-call ≤ budget
Worked example
  1. Maximize revenue: 5x + 4y, where x, y are two rep types deployed
  2. Constraints: x + y ≤ 10 (capacity), x ≤ 6, y ≤ 7, x,y ≥ 0
  3. Evaluate the feasible region's corner points: (0,0)→0, (6,0)→30, (0,7)→28, (6,4)→5(6)+4(4)=30+16=46, (3,7)→15+28=43
Maximum at (x=6, y=4): revenue = 46 — the optimal solution always sits at a corner of the feasible region, which is exactly what a solver checks systematically instead of guessing.
Daily-life analogyPacking a suitcase under a weight limit — choosing which items maximize value without exceeding capacity is a mini-LP problem.
Consumer exampleA meal-delivery app deciding how many of each dish to prep given kitchen capacity and demand forecasts.
Business exampleAllocating a fixed budget across marketing channels to maximize expected leads.
Enterprise exampleAirlines solving crew-scheduling and seat-pricing as large-scale LP/IP problems daily.
Interview example"How would you decide the optimal rep count?" — define the objective, decision variables, and constraints exactly like this, then solve.
Your CVThis is the literal structure behind the confirmed LP-plus-heuristics allocation of ~60 reps at Axtria, built out fully in Stage 9.

Descriptive Statistics

The vocabulary every other section assumes you already have cold.

Central tendency A

Mean — the average; sensitive to outliers. Median — the middle value; robust to outliers. Mode — the most frequent value; the only one that works on categorical data.

Interview trap: "average revenue per user" can be badly misleading if a handful of enterprise accounts skew it — that's exactly when you reach for median instead, and naming that tradeoff unprompted signals real fluency.

Worked example
  1. 10 deal sizes ($K): 39, 40, 40, 41, 42, 42, 43, 44, 45, 500
  2. Mean = (39+40+40+41+42+42+43+44+45+500)/10 = 876/10 = 87.6
  3. Median = average of 5th and 6th sorted values = (42+42)/2 = 42
Mean = $87.6K, Median = $42K — one $500K enterprise deal drags the mean far above what a "typical" deal actually looks like. Reporting only the mean here would badly mislead a stakeholder.
Daily-life analogy"Average" salary at a small company sounds high the moment the founder's salary is included — the median employee's pay tells a truer story.
Consumer example"Average" home price in a neighborhood can be skewed by one mansion; median price is what a typical buyer should expect.
Business exampleAverage deal size skewed by one enterprise account — exactly the example above.
Enterprise exampleExecutive dashboards often report both mean and median revenue per account specifically to avoid this distortion.
Interview example"Why report median instead of mean here?" — because the distribution is skewed by outliers, and median better represents the typical case.
Your CVCost-per-call and rep efficiency metrics at Axtria are exactly the kind of business metric worth checking for skew before summarizing with a single number.

Spread A

Variance — average squared distance from the mean. Standard deviation — the square root of variance, back in the original units, which is why it's the one people actually report.

σ² = Σ(xᵢ − μ)² / n
Worked example
  1. 5 call durations (minutes): 4, 6, 5, 7, 3 → mean = 25/5 = 5
  2. Deviations from mean: −1, 1, 0, 2, −2
  3. Squared deviations: 1, 1, 0, 4, 4 → sum = 10
  4. Population variance = 10/5 = 2; standard deviation = √2 ≈ 1.41
σ ≈ 1.41 minutes — the typical call duration deviates from the 5-minute average by about a minute and a half.
Daily-life analogyTwo commutes can both average 30 minutes, but one is always 28-32 while the other swings 10-50 — same mean, very different variance.
Business exampleTwo reps with the same average call-handling time can have very different consistency — variance is what separates "reliable" from "erratic but averages out."
Interview example"Why do we square the deviations instead of just averaging them?" — plain deviations sum to zero by definition of the mean; squaring makes them all positive so they don't cancel out.
Your CVCost-per-call variance across reps at Axtria is exactly what "rep efficiency modeling" needs to account for — a low-average, high-variance rep is a different problem than a high-average, low-variance one.

Shape & outliers B

Skewness tells you if the distribution leans — right-skewed data (like income or cost-per-call) has a long tail of high values, which is exactly the kind of distribution that makes "mean" misleading. Outliers get flagged with IQR (values beyond 1.5× the interquartile range) or z-score thresholds.

Worked example
  1. 9 sorted values: 12, 14, 15, 16, 18, 19, 20, 21, 95
  2. Q1 (25th percentile, position 2.5) ≈ interpolate between 14, 15 → 14.5
  3. Q3 (75th percentile, position 6.5) ≈ interpolate between 19, 20 → 19.5
  4. IQR = 19.5 − 14.5 = 5. Upper fence = Q3 + 1.5×IQR = 19.5 + 7.5 = 27
95 > 27 → flagged as an outlier by the IQR rule, and worth investigating rather than silently averaging in.
Daily-life analogyOne flight delayed by 10 hours shouldn't define "typical delay" for an airline that's usually on time — that's an outlier, not the norm.
Business exampleOne giant order in a batch of transaction data can distort average order value unless flagged and handled deliberately.
Interview example"How do you decide whether to remove an outlier?" — check the cause first: a data-entry error is safe to fix or drop; a real extreme business event usually should stay or be analyzed separately.
Your CVThe NanoCloud "50+ network heatmaps" data almost certainly needed exactly this kind of outlier screening before being converted into coverage recommendations.

Probability Distributions

The three you'll actually be asked about, and when each one is the right model for real data.

Normal (Gaussian) A

Symmetric bell curve. Applies whenever a quantity is the sum of many small independent effects — heights, measurement error, and (per the CLT below) sample means in general, even when the underlying data isn't normal itself.

68-95-99.7 rule: ~68% of values fall within 1 std dev of the mean, ~95% within 2, ~99.7% within 3 — see it live below.

Worked example
  1. Call durations are approximately normal: mean = 5.0 min, SD = 1.2 min
  2. What fraction of calls last longer than 7.4 minutes?
  3. z = (7.4 − 5.0) / 1.2 = 2.0
  4. By the 68-95-99.7 rule, 95% of values fall within ±2 SD, leaving 5% split across both tails
≈ 2.5% of calls last longer than 7.4 minutes — the single upper tail beyond +2 SD.
Daily-life analogyAdult heights cluster tightly around an average with fewer and fewer people the further you get from it in either direction.
Business exampleCall durations, deal cycle times, and measurement error in almost any process tend toward this shape.
Interview example"What percent of values fall beyond 2 standard deviations?" — about 5% total, 2.5% per tail, straight from the empirical rule.
Your CVFlagging unusually long or short calls at Axtria for review is exactly this z-score logic in practice.

Binomial B

Counts successes across a fixed number of independent yes/no trials. This is the exact distribution behind A/B test conversion rates — "how many of these 1,000 visitors converted" is a binomial question.

P(X=k) = C(n,k)·pᵏ·(1−p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ
Worked example
  1. 10 sales calls, each with p = 0.3 probability of converting, independently
  2. P(exactly 3 conversions) = C(10,3) × 0.3³ × 0.7⁷
  3. C(10,3) = 120; 0.3³ = 0.027; 0.7⁷ ≈ 0.0824
  4. 120 × 0.027 × 0.0824 ≈ 0.267
≈ 26.7% chance of exactly 3 conversions out of 10 calls at a 30% true conversion rate.
Daily-life analogyFlipping a biased coin 10 times and asking the chance of exactly 3 heads.
Business exampleModeling how many of a batch of sales calls convert, given a known base conversion rate.
Interview example"Why binomial and not normal here?" — discrete, fixed number of independent yes/no trials; normal is a continuous approximation that only works well for larger n.
Your CVA/B test conversion counts (CV competency: A/B Testing) are fundamentally binomial data before any normal-approximation test is applied to them.

Poisson C

Counts rare events over a fixed interval of time or space, e.g. support tickets per hour, network failures per day — relevant background for the NanoCloud downtime/turnaround claims.

P(X=k) = λᵏe⁻λ / k!
Worked example
  1. Average λ = 3 network incidents per week
  2. P(exactly 5 incidents in a week) = e⁻³ × 3⁵ / 5!
  3. e⁻³ ≈ 0.0498; 3⁵ = 243; 5! = 120
  4. 0.0498 × 243 / 120 ≈ 0.1008
≈ 10.1% chance of exactly 5 incidents in a week that normally averages 3 — useful context for deciding whether a "bad week" is genuinely unusual.
Daily-life analogyNumber of emails you receive in an hour — rare-ish, countable events over a fixed window.
Business exampleSupport tickets per day, or network incidents per week, for capacity/staffing planning.
Interview example"When is Poisson wrong to use?" — when events aren't independent (one failure triggers cascading failures) or the rate isn't stable over the interval.
Your CVNanoCloud's downtime and turnaround-time claims are exactly the kind of metric a Poisson framing would sit behind operationally.

Central Limit Theorem — deep dive

The single most-tested statistics concept in analytics interviews, because it's the reason hypothesis testing is allowed to exist at all.

What it says: take repeated samples of size n from any population — no matter how skewed or weird its shape — and the distribution of the sample means approaches a Normal distribution as n grows, centered on the true population mean.

Why it exists / the problem it solves: without it, every statistical test would need a different formula for every possible underlying data shape. The CLT is what lets one framework (z-tests, t-tests, confidence intervals) apply broadly, because it's making a claim about the sampling distribution of the mean, not about the raw data's shape.

Intuition: imagine rolling a single die — the outcomes (1–6) are uniformly spread, not remotely bell-shaped. But if you roll 5 dice and take the average, and repeat that thousands of times, the distribution of those averages piles up in the middle and thins at the edges — a bell curve emerges purely from averaging, even though no single die roll is ever "bell-shaped." That's the CLT, and you can watch it happen below.

Worked example — how sample size shrinks the standard error
  1. A population of call durations has true standard deviation σ = 10 minutes
  2. Standard error of the sample mean: SE = σ/√n
  3. At n=25: SE = 10/√25 = 10/5 = 2.0
  4. At n=100: SE = 10/√100 = 10/10 = 1.0
  5. At n=400: SE = 10/√400 = 10/20 = 0.5
Quadrupling the sample size halves the standard error — this is exactly why larger samples give tighter, more trustworthy confidence intervals, and why the CLT's "approaches normal, with shrinking spread" is a quantifiable, not just qualitative, claim.
Daily-life analogyAny single commute takes a wildly different time day to day, but your average commute time over a month settles into a predictable, bell-shaped range.
Consumer exampleOne restaurant review can be a 1-star outlier; the average of thousands of reviews is stable and bell-shaped around the restaurant's true quality.
Business exampleDaily revenue is noisy and skewed, but the average of 30 days of revenue behaves predictably — which is why monthly/quarterly reporting is more stable than daily.
Enterprise exampleA telecom network's per-second latency is erratic, but average latency over an hour, aggregated across thousands of sessions, is what SLAs are actually built on.
Interview example"Our data isn't normal — can we still use a t-test?" — yes, because the CLT applies to the sampling distribution of the mean, not the raw data, once n is reasonably large (rule of thumb: n≥30).
Your CVThe Axtria forecasting model's cost-per-call and rep-efficiency metrics are averaged across reps and time periods — the CLT is exactly why those averages can be trusted and statistically tested, even though individual calls vary wildly.

Sampling & Confidence Intervals

A confidence interval is a range built from your sample that's designed to contain the true population value a known percentage of the time across repeated sampling — a 95% CI means: if you repeated this study infinitely, 95% of the intervals you'd construct would contain the true value. It does not mean "there's a 95% probability the true value is in this specific interval" — the true value is fixed, not random; the interval is what varies from sample to sample. This exact distinction is a favorite interview trap.

CI = x̄ ± z · (σ / √n)

Business framing: "average deal size is $42K ± $3K at 95% confidence" is a far more honest and useful statement to a stakeholder than a single point estimate, because it communicates the uncertainty a point estimate hides.

Worked example
  1. Sample of n=100 reps, average cost-per-call x̄ = $18.50, sample standard deviation σ = $6.00
  2. Standard error: SE = σ/√n = 6.00/√100 = 6.00/10 = 0.60
  3. For 95% confidence, z = 1.96
  4. Margin of error: 1.96 × 0.60 = 1.176 ≈ 1.18
  5. CI = 18.50 ± 1.18
95% CI = [$17.32, $19.68] — "we're 95% confident the true average cost-per-call across all reps lies between $17.32 and $19.68," not a claim about any single rep.
Daily-life analogyA weather forecast of "70–75°F tomorrow" is a confidence interval — a range the true temperature is expected to fall in, not one exact promised number.
Consumer example"Delivery in 20–30 minutes" is functionally a confidence interval on delivery time, built from historical delivery data.
Business example"We expect $17.32–$19.68 average cost-per-call" is more honest to a client than a single number that implies false precision.
Enterprise exampleSLA commitments ("99.9% uptime ± monitoring margin") are built from confidence intervals over historical performance data.
Interview example"Your CI is really wide — what does that tell you?" — either high variance in the underlying data, or too small a sample; both are legitimate answers, and naming which one applies shows real understanding.
Your CVThe $10M revenue figure and the 28% NanoCloud performance improvement are point estimates — being ready to frame either with an honest uncertainty range, if asked, is stronger than presenting them as exact.

Hypothesis Testing Framework

The exact reasoning skeleton behind every A/B test, every "is this real or noise" question, and the framework Stage 10 builds on directly.

Null & Alternative Hypotheses A

H₀ (null): the default, "nothing changed" position — e.g. the new page design has no effect on conversion. H₁ (alternative): the effect you're trying to find evidence for. You never "prove" H₀ true — you either reject it or fail to reject it.

Worked example
  1. H₀: a new self-service dashboard has no effect on average decision time (true mean = 15 min); H₁: mean ≠ 15 min
  2. Sample: n=36 stakeholders, observed mean = 12.8 min, sample SD = 4.2 min
  3. t = (12.8 − 15) / (4.2/√36) = −2.2 / 0.70 ≈ −3.14
  4. Critical t at df=35, α=0.05 two-tailed ≈ ±2.03
|−3.14| > 2.03 → reject H₀. The dashboard is associated with a statistically significant reduction in decision time, not just a random fluctuation around 15 minutes.
Daily-life analogy"This new route isn't actually faster" is your default assumption (H₀) until enough trips convince you otherwise.
Business exampleDid the new self-service tool actually speed up decisions — exactly the example above.
Interview example"Why start by assuming no effect?" — it's the conservative, skeptical default; the burden of proof is on the data to show a real effect, not on the null to prove nothing happened.
Your CVThe Axtria self-service decision-support tool bullet is a natural place to have this exact hypothesis-test framing ready.

p-value A

The probability of seeing a result at least as extreme as what you observed, if H₀ were true. A small p-value means your data would be surprising under the null — it does not mean "probability the null is true," and it does not mean "probability your result is due to chance." Both are the single most common p-value misinterpretation, and interviewers ask this specifically to check for it.

Worked example
  1. H₀: the new outreach script has no effect on call-to-conversion rate
  2. Control (old script): 400 calls, 48 conversions → p̂A = 0.120
  3. Treatment (new script): 400 calls, 68 conversions → p̂B = 0.170
  4. Pooled proportion: (48+68)/800 = 0.145; SE = √[0.145×0.855×(1/400+1/400)] ≈ 0.0250
  5. z = (0.170 − 0.120) / 0.0250 = 0.050 / 0.0250 = 2.00
  6. Two-tailed p-value for z=2.00 ≈ 0.0455
p ≈ 0.046 — below the standard 0.05 threshold, so you'd reject H₀: if the script truly had no effect, seeing a gap this large (or larger) would happen only ~4.6% of the time by chance.
Daily-life analogyFlipping a coin 20 times and getting 17 heads is surprising if the coin is fair — the p-value quantifies exactly how surprising.
Consumer exampleA supplement brand claims "clinically proven" — the p-value behind that claim tells you how surprising the trial's results would be if the supplement actually did nothing.
Business exampleA new outreach script raising conversion from 12% to 17%, p=0.046 — likely a real effect, not noise, at the standard 5% threshold.
Enterprise examplePharma trials (relevant to Axtria's industry context) require pre-registered significance thresholds precisely because p-values are easy to misuse if you test repeatedly until one looks good.
Interview example"p=0.06 — is that not significant, so no effect?" — it's above the conventional 0.05 cutoff, but that's a somewhat arbitrary line; report the actual value and effect size rather than a binary yes/no.
Your CVAny claim that the Axtria forecasting model's output "replaced manual reporting" implicitly assumes its predictions were validated as better-than-chance — this is the exact test that claim rests on.

Type I & Type II Error A

Type I (α): rejecting H₀ when it's actually true — a false positive (you ship a "winning" variant that wasn't really better). Type II (β): failing to reject H₀ when it's actually false — a false negative (you miss a real effect). They trade off against each other for a fixed sample size.

Worked example
  1. 1,000 flagged transactions reviewed; true fraud rate is 5% → 50 actually fraudulent, 950 actually legitimate
  2. Test has 90% power → detects 45 of the 50 real fraud cases, misses 5 (Type II errors)
  3. Test has a 5% false-positive rate on legitimate transactions → flags 0.05×950 ≈ 47.5 legitimate transactions incorrectly (Type I errors)
≈ 45 true catches, ≈ 5 missed fraud cases (Type II), ≈ 48 false alarms on innocent transactions (Type I) — a concrete count behind the abstract error-rate percentages.
Daily-life analogyA smoke detector that's too sensitive gives false alarms (Type I); one that's not sensitive enough misses real fires (Type II).
Business exampleFraud detection thresholds — exactly the example above.
Interview example"Which error is worse here?" — depends on the cost: missing real fraud (Type II) vs. annoying legitimate customers with false flags (Type I); the "right" threshold depends on that cost tradeoff, not a fixed rule.
Your CVThe Axtria forecasting model's rep-allocation decisions carry exactly this tradeoff — allocating to the wrong rep (false positive) vs. missing an efficient one (false negative).

Power & Effect Size B

Power (1−β) is the probability of correctly detecting a real effect — typically targeted at 80%. Effect size is how big the difference actually is, independent of sample size — a huge sample can make a trivially small, meaningless effect "statistically significant," which is exactly why effect size matters alongside the p-value.

Worked example — Cohen's d
  1. Group A (old training): mean score = 20, SD = 5
  2. Group B (new training): mean score = 25, SD = 5
  3. Cohen's d = (mean B − mean A) / pooled SD = (25 − 20) / 5 = 1.0
d = 1.0 is conventionally a "large" effect (0.2=small, 0.5=medium, 0.8+=large) — a big enough true effect that even a fairly small sample can detect it at 80% power, unlike a d=0.1 effect which would need a much larger sample.
Daily-life analogyA tiny, barely-noticeable taste difference needs a huge blind taste-test panel to detect reliably; an obvious difference needs only a handful of tasters.
Business exampleComparing training program effectiveness on rep performance scores — exactly the example above.
Interview example"Your test wasn't significant — was there no effect?" — could mean no real effect, or could mean the study was underpowered to detect a real but modest effect size; these are different conclusions.
Your CVBefore running any A/B test on Axtria's tools, sizing the required sample against an expected/minimum meaningful effect avoids wasting a stakeholder review cycle on an underpowered test.

Test Selector — t-test, z-test, χ², ANOVA

The four tests that come up constantly. Click each to expand the full breakdown — question answered, assumptions, hypotheses, business example, and a runnable Python snippet.

t-testCompare means of two groups, small/unknown-variance samples
Question it answersIs the mean of Group A different from Group B?
When to useComparing two group means (e.g. two A/B variants) when population variance is unknown — the common real-world case
AssumptionsRoughly normal data (or large enough n via CLT), independent observations, similar variances (Welch's t-test relaxes this last one)
H₀ / H₁H₀: μA = μB  |  H₁: μA ≠ μB (two-sided) or μA > μB (one-sided)
Test statistict = (x̄A − x̄B) / SE(difference)
Business exampleDid the new pricing page increase average order value?
Python
from scipy import stats t_stat, p_value = stats.ttest_ind(group_a, group_b, equal_var=False) print(f"t={t_stat:.3f}, p={p_value:.4f}")
Worked example
  1. Page A (old): n=15 sessions, average order value x̄A = $64, sA = $12
  2. Page B (new): n=15 sessions, average order value x̄B = $71, sB = $13
  3. SE(diff) ≈ √(12²/15 + 13²/15) = √(9.6 + 11.3) = √20.9 ≈ 4.57
  4. t = (71 − 64) / 4.57 ≈ 1.53
  5. df ≈ 27 (Welch approx.); two-tailed p for t=1.53 ≈ 0.137
p ≈ 0.137 — above 0.05, so with this sample size you can't conclude Page B truly increased order value; a $7 gap this large could plausibly happen from sampling noise alone. The honest read: promising trend, underpowered sample.
Daily-life analogyComparing average commute time between two routes you've each driven a handful of times — a few trips isn't enough to be sure one route is truly faster.
Business exampleDid the redesigned checkout page raise average order value? Exactly the scenario above.
Interview example"Why not just compare the two averages directly?" — because any gap could be sampling noise; the t-test quantifies how likely a gap this large is if there's truly no difference.
Your CVComparing cost-per-call or rep efficiency before/after a process change at Axtria or NanoCloud is a natural t-test scenario.
z-testCompare proportions or means with known variance, large samples
Question it answersIs this proportion/mean different from a known benchmark or another group?
When to useLarge samples (n > ~30) with known or well-estimated population variance — the standard test for A/B conversion rate comparisons
AssumptionsLarge sample size, known population variance (or large-n approximation), independent observations
H₀ / H₁H₀: pA = pB  |  H₁: pA ≠ pB
Test statisticz = (p̂A − p̂B) / SE(difference)
Business exampleDid the new checkout flow increase conversion rate from 3.1% to 3.6%?
Python
from statsmodels.stats.proportion import proportions_ztest count = [312, 360] # conversions nobs = [10000, 10000] # visitors z_stat, p_value = proportions_ztest(count, nobs) print(f"z={z_stat:.3f}, p={p_value:.4f}")
Worked example (full calculation in §07 p-value card above)
  1. Control: 400 calls, 48 conversions (12.0%) vs. Treatment: 400 calls, 68 conversions (17.0%)
  2. z ≈ 2.00, two-tailed p ≈ 0.046
Same underlying math as the p-value example above — a z-test is simply a hypothesis test framed with the z-statistic. Reject H₀ at the 5% level.
Daily-life analogyComparing the free-throw percentage of two basketball players across a full season (large n) — z-tests fit large-sample proportion comparisons.
Business exampleDid a new outreach script's conversion rate genuinely beat the old one, at scale?
Interview example"Why z-test here and not t-test?" — large sample, comparing proportions with a well-approximated variance under CLT, not small-sample means.
Your CVThe A/B Testing competency on every CV version is most often literally this test in production.
Chi-square (χ²)Test relationships between categorical variables
Question it answersAre two categorical variables independent, or is there a relationship?
When to useCategorical vs. categorical (e.g. device type vs. converted/not-converted) — a contingency-table test, not a means comparison
AssumptionsIndependent observations, expected cell counts ≥ 5 in most cells
H₀ / H₁H₀: the two variables are independent  |  H₁: they're associated
Test statisticχ² = Σ (Observed − Expected)² / Expected
Business exampleDoes device type (mobile/desktop) relate to which pricing plan customers pick?
Python
from scipy.stats import chi2_contingency chi2, p_value, dof, expected = chi2_contingency(contingency_table) print(f"chi2={chi2:.3f}, p={p_value:.4f}")
Worked example
  1. 200 mobile users: 30 chose Plan A, 70 chose Plan B → row total 100
  2. 200 desktop users: 55 chose Plan A, 45 chose Plan B → row total 100
  3. Column totals: Plan A = 85, Plan B = 115, Grand total = 200
  4. Expected(mobile, A) = (100×85)/200 = 42.5; Expected(mobile, B) = 57.5; Expected(desktop, A) = 42.5; Expected(desktop, B) = 57.5
  5. χ² = Σ(O−E)²/E = (30−42.5)²/42.5 + (70−57.5)²/57.5 + (55−42.5)²/42.5 + (45−57.5)²/57.5 ≈ 3.68+2.72+3.68+2.72 = 12.79
  6. df = (2−1)(2−1) = 1; p-value for χ²=12.79, df=1 ≈ 0.00035
p ≈ 0.0003 — strong evidence device type and plan choice are associated; mobile users skew toward Plan B, desktop users toward Plan A.
Daily-life analogyChecking whether people's ice-cream flavor choice differs by which neighborhood they live in — two categorical variables, is there a relationship?
Business exampleDoes device type relate to which pricing plan customers pick — exactly the example above.
Interview example"Your expected cell count is 3 — is that a problem?" — yes, chi-square gets unreliable below ~5 expected per cell; consider Fisher's exact test instead for small samples.
Your CVSegmenting NanoCloud client outcomes by client size/industry to see if satisfaction or approval rates differ by category is a natural chi-square use.
ANOVACompare means across three or more groups
Question it answersIs at least one group mean different among 3+ groups?
When to useMore than two groups (e.g. A/B/C testing three page variants at once) — avoids the inflated false-positive rate from running many pairwise t-tests
AssumptionsNormality within groups, independent observations, similar variances across groups (homoscedasticity)
H₀ / H₁H₀: μ1 = μ2 = μ3  |  H₁: at least one mean differs
Test statisticF = (variance between groups) / (variance within groups)
Business exampleDo three different email subject lines produce different open rates?
Python
from scipy import stats f_stat, p_value = stats.f_oneway(group_a, group_b, group_c) print(f"F={f_stat:.3f}, p={p_value:.4f}") # significant? -> run a post-hoc test (Tukey HSD) to see WHICH pair differs
Worked example (simplified, equal group sizes)
  1. Subject line A: open rates across 5 sends — 20%, 22%, 19%, 21%, 23% → mean = 21%
  2. Subject line B: 25%, 27%, 24%, 26%, 28% → mean = 26%
  3. Subject line C: 20%, 21%, 19%, 22%, 20% → mean = 20.4%
  4. Grand mean = (21+26+20.4)/3 ≈ 22.47%
  5. Between-group variance is driven mainly by B sitting well above A and C; within-group variance is small (each group is tightly clustered)
  6. F = (variance between groups) / (variance within groups) works out large here because between-group spread dominates within-group noise → F ≈ 24.8, p < 0.001
p < 0.001 — at least one subject line differs. A Tukey HSD post-hoc test would then show B significantly beats both A and C, while A vs. C isn't significantly different.
Daily-life analogyComparing average commute time across three different departure times, not just two — ANOVA is the "more than two groups" version of a t-test.
Business exampleDo three different email subject lines produce different open rates — exactly the example above.
Interview example"Why not just run three separate t-tests?" — because each test at 5% alpha compounds the false-positive risk across multiple comparisons; ANOVA tests all groups in one pass.
Your CVComparing network performance improvement across 3+ NanoCloud client segments, or rep-efficiency across multiple sales regions at Axtria, is a natural ANOVA scenario.

Correlation, Covariance & Causation

Covariance B

Direction of the relationship between two variables — positive, negative, or none — but the magnitude isn't interpretable on its own because it's in the product of the two variables' units.

Worked example
  1. Marketing spend x ($K): 10, 20, 30, 40, 50 → mean = 30
  2. Revenue y ($K): 15, 18, 25, 25, 32 → mean = 23
  3. Deviations x: −20,−10,0,10,20  |  Deviations y: −8,−5,2,2,9
  4. Products: 160, 50, 0, 20, 180 → sum = 410
  5. Covariance = 410/5 = 82
Cov = 82 (in $K×$K units) — positive, so spend and revenue move together, but this raw number alone doesn't tell you how strong the relationship is. That's what correlation (next card) fixes.

Correlation (r) A

Covariance, normalized to a unitless −1 to +1 scale, so magnitude becomes meaningfully comparable. r = 0.8 between marketing spend and revenue is a strong linear association — but association is not causation.

Worked example (continuing from covariance above)
  1. Var(x) = (400+100+0+100+400)/5 = 1000/5 = 200 → SD(x) = √200 ≈ 14.14
  2. Var(y) = (64+25+4+4+81)/5 = 178/5 = 35.6 → SD(y) ≈ 5.97
  3. r = Cov(x,y) / (SD(x)×SD(y)) = 82 / (14.14×5.97) = 82 / 84.4
r ≈ 0.97 — a very strong positive linear relationship between marketing spend and revenue in this small dataset.
Daily-life analogyHours studied and exam score usually move together — more of one tends to come with more of the other.
Business exampleMarketing spend and revenue — exactly the example above.
Interview example"r=0.97 — is this a good model?" — a strong correlation isn't the same as a validated causal driver; still needs the causation check on the next card.
Your CVCost-per-call vs. rep efficiency at Axtria is exactly the kind of pair worth correlating before building it into the allocation model.

Correlation ≠ Causation A

Ice cream sales and drowning deaths correlate — heat is the confounder driving both. Any correlation-based claim needs to survive "what's the confounder?" before it becomes a business recommendation.

Worked example
  1. Temperature (°C): 20, 25, 30, 35, 40
  2. Ice cream sales (units): 100, 150, 220, 280, 350 — rises with temperature
  3. Drownings (count): 2, 3, 5, 6, 9 — also rises with temperature
  4. Computing r(ice cream, drownings) directly, using the same method as the correlation card above, gives r ≈ 0.99
A near-perfect correlation between ice cream sales and drownings — with zero causal link. Both are driven by the same third variable (temperature: more people swim and more people buy ice cream on hot days). The math doesn't distinguish "confounded" from "causal" — only domain reasoning or an experiment can.
Daily-life analogyPeople who carry umbrellas are more likely to see rain — the umbrella doesn't cause the rain; both are driven by the forecast/clouds.
Consumer example"Customers who read reviews buy more" — do reviews cause purchases, or do already-interested buyers just read more reviews (reverse causation)?
Business exampleReps with more training hours close more deals — could be training's effect, or your best reps simply choose to train more (selection, not causation).
Enterprise exampleA/B testing exists specifically to move from correlation to causation at scale, by randomizing the one variable you're testing and holding everything else constant.
Interview example"Your model shows X correlates with churn — should we intervene on X?" — only if there's a causal mechanism, ideally validated by an experiment, not just an observed correlation.
Your CVThe Axtria forecasting model informing "quarterly market-share strategy" is a correlation-to-decision pipeline — worth being ready to explain what validates the causal read, not just the statistical association.

Multicollinearity B

When predictor variables are highly correlated with each other, regression coefficients become unstable and hard to interpret individually — a direct callback for Stage 7's Logistic Regression module, where this is a named interview trap.

Worked example — VIF and coefficient instability
  1. Predictors: years of experience (x1) and age (x2), correlated at r=0.95 with each other
  2. Regressing x1 on the other predictors gives R² = 0.90 for that sub-model
  3. VIF(x1) = 1 / (1 − R²) = 1 / (1 − 0.90) = 1 / 0.10 = 10
  4. Rule of thumb: VIF > 5–10 signals problematic multicollinearity
VIF=10 flags a real problem: on slightly different samples, this model's fitted coefficients might swing from x1=+3.2, x2=+0.1 to x1=−1.8, x2=+4.9 — wildly different individual coefficients, even though their combined effect stays roughly stable. Individually, neither coefficient can be trusted or interpreted.
Daily-life analogyIf height and shoe size are both in a model predicting basketball skill, it becomes hard to say which one "really" matters — they move together.
Business exampleYears of experience and age in a compensation model — nearly redundant predictors that destabilize individual coefficient interpretation.
Interview example"Your logistic regression coefficient flipped sign after adding a feature — why?" — classic multicollinearity symptom; check VIF before trusting any individual coefficient's story.
Your CVRep efficiency and cost-per-call at Axtria could plausibly be correlated with each other — worth being ready to say how that was checked before both went into the model.

Statistical vs. Business Significance

A large enough sample can make a 0.1% conversion lift statistically significant — real, not noise — while still being far too small to justify the engineering cost of shipping it. Statistical significance answers "is this effect real?"; business significance answers "is this effect big enough to matter?" A strong interview answer always addresses both, not just the p-value.

Worked example
  1. Conversion A = 5.00%, Conversion B = 5.05%, n = 5,000,000 visitors per group
  2. SE(difference) = √[2 × 0.05 × 0.95 / 5,000,000] ≈ √(0.000000019) ≈ 0.000138
  3. z = 0.0005 / 0.000138 ≈ 3.62 → p ≈ 0.0003 — statistically significant
Real and statistically significant — but a 0.05 percentage-point lift. Whether that's worth shipping depends entirely on the absolute revenue it represents at your scale, not on the p-value alone.
Daily-life analogyA diet proven to make you lose "a statistically significant" 0.1 lb over a year isn't a diet worth following, even if the effect is real.
Business exampleA 0.05pp conversion lift at massive scale — exactly the example above.
Interview example"Your test is significant — should we ship it?" — ask about effect size and implementation cost before answering yes.
Your CVThe $10M Axtria outcome is exactly the kind of number that should be framed with both statistical validity and business magnitude, not treated as self-evidently important because it's large.

Red Flags — statements that expose shallow knowledge

"The p-value is the probability the null hypothesis is true." — Wrong. It's the probability of the observed data (or more extreme), assuming H₀ is true. The null is either true or false; it doesn't have a probability in frequentist testing.
"It's statistically significant, so it matters." — Significance and business impact are different questions; always report effect size alongside p-value.
"Correlation of 0.9, so X causes Y." — Needs a confounder check, ideally a controlled experiment, before any causal claim.
"95% confidence interval means 95% probability the true value is in this range." — The true value is fixed; it's the interval-construction procedure that's correct 95% of the time across repeated sampling.
"We didn't find significance, so there's no effect." — Failing to reject H₀ isn't proof H₀ is true — it may just mean the sample was underpowered to detect a real, smaller effect.
