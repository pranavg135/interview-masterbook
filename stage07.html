03

Linear Regression

The foundation, its assumptions, what breaks them, and the regularized/nonlinear alternatives.

Simple & Multiple Linear Regression (OLS) A

Fits a line (or hyperplane, with multiple predictors) minimizing the sum of squared residuals (Ordinary Least Squares). Each coefficient represents the expected change in the target for a one-unit change in that predictor, holding others constant.

Worked example
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression # Predict call_value from duration and rep_tenure X = calls_df[["duration_min", "rep_tenure_years"]] y = calls_df["call_value"] model = LinearRegression().fit(X, y) print(model.intercept_, model.coef_) # intercept: 42.1, coef: [8.3, 15.6] # interpretation: each extra minute of call duration is # associated with +$8.30 in call value, holding tenure fixed; # each extra year of rep tenure is associated with +$15.60
The coefficients are directly interpretable in the target's real units — this is Linear Regression's biggest practical advantage over more complex models, and exactly why it's still chosen even when it isn't the most accurate option available.
Daily-life"Each extra hour of studying is associated with 4 more points on the test" — a direct, additive, interpretable relationship.
ConsumerA real-estate price estimator explaining "$50/sqft, +$10K per bedroom" in plain, additive terms.
BusinessEstimating how each additional sales call or marketing dollar is associated with revenue, in directly interpretable units.
EnterpriseA plausible early/baseline step in the Axtria forecasting or call-value modeling work before moving to more complex models.
Interview"Why would you ever use Linear Regression over a more powerful model?" — interpretability, speed, and a strong baseline to justify whether added complexity is actually worth it.
Your CVDirectly ties to the "regression modeling" language across your CVs — solid, honest Level A/B foundation.

Assumptions — and What Happens If Violated A

Linearity (the true relationship is linear in the parameters), independence of errors, homoscedasticity (constant error variance), normality of residuals, and no severe multicollinearity. Violations don't always break predictions, but they invalidate the model's statistical inference (p-values, confidence intervals).

Worked example
# Checking homoscedasticity: plot residuals vs. fitted values residuals = y_test - model.predict(X_test) # A funnel shape (residual spread growing with fitted value) # signals heteroscedasticity — e.g. call value predictions get # less precise for very high-value calls # Fix options: log-transform the target, use weighted least # squares, or use a model that doesn't assume constant variance
Heteroscedasticity doesn't necessarily bias the coefficient estimates themselves, but it does bias the standard errors — meaning your confidence intervals and significance tests become unreliable even if the point predictions are still reasonable.
Daily-lifeA bathroom scale that's precise for normal weights but wildly inconsistent at extremes — the "error" isn't constant across the range.
ConsumerA shipping-time estimator that's accurate for standard orders but erratic for large bulk orders — heteroscedastic error.
BusinessRevenue predictions that are tight for small accounts but wildly variable for enterprise accounts — worth flagging to stakeholders as a limitation.
EnterpriseA realistic, honest thing to flag if Axtria's call-value regression showed less-reliable predictions at the high end — a genuinely sophisticated diagnostic observation.
Interview"How would you check if your linear model's assumptions hold?" — residual plots (vs. fitted values, and a Q-Q plot for normality), and a VIF check for multicollinearity.
CV-specificA strong Level A answer regardless of whether this exact diagnostic was performed at Axtria — the reasoning is what interviewers are testing.

Gradient Descent vs. the Normal Equation B

Linear Regression's optimal coefficients can be solved exactly via the Normal Equation (a closed-form matrix formula) for small-to-medium data, or iteratively via Gradient Descent (stepping downhill on the loss surface), which scales better to very large or high-dimensional data.

Worked example
# Normal equation (closed-form): β = (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀy import numpy as np X_b = np.c_[np.ones((len(X),1)), X] # add intercept column beta = np.linalg.inv(X_b.T @ X_b) @ X_b.T @ y print(beta) # matches sklearn's LinearRegression coefficients exactly # Gradient descent (iterative): converges to the same answer # but scales to millions of rows / features where inverting # (XᵀX) becomes computationally too expensive
Both methods converge to the identical optimal coefficients on well-behaved data — the choice is purely about computational scalability, not about getting a different or "better" answer.
Daily-lifeSolving an equation with algebra directly (normal equation) vs. guessing and adjusting your guess step by step until it's close enough (gradient descent).
ConsumerAny large-scale recommender system trains via gradient descent, not a closed-form matrix inversion — the data is far too large.
BusinessA small quarterly regression on 200 rows: normal equation is instant. A model trained on millions of transaction rows: gradient descent (or a variant) is the only practical choice.
EnterpriseAt Axtria's moderate batch scale (Stage 5), either approach would work — but it's the right conceptual answer to explain both and when each applies.
Interview"Why not always use the normal equation since it's exact?" — inverting (XᵀX) is O(n³) in the number of features and becomes computationally infeasible at high dimensionality or very large data.
CV-specificGood Level B/C depth — shows understanding of what's happening under sklearn's .fit() call, not just how to call it.

Polynomial Regression B

Extends linear regression by adding polynomial terms (x², x³, ...) as new features — still a linear model in its parameters, but can now fit curved relationships. Risk: higher-degree polynomials overfit quickly (Stage 6's bias-variance tradeoff in action).

Worked example
from sklearn.preprocessing import PolynomialFeatures from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline # Call value may rise then plateau with duration — a curve, # not a straight line poly_model = make_pipeline(PolynomialFeatures(degree=2), LinearRegression()) poly_model.fit(X_train[["duration_min"]], y_train) # degree=2 lets the model capture a rise-then-plateau shape # that plain linear regression structurally cannot represent
A degree-2 term lets the model bend to fit a genuine plateau effect — but jumping to degree=8 on the same data would start fitting noise between individual points, a direct instance of the overfitting pattern from Stage 6.
Daily-lifeDiminishing returns — the 5th cup of coffee doesn't help as much as the 1st; a straight line can't capture that curve, a polynomial term can.
ConsumerModeling how ad exposure frequency relates to purchase likelihood — rises, then plateaus or even declines (ad fatigue).
BusinessDiminishing returns on marketing spend or sales call duration — a genuine nonlinear pattern worth modeling explicitly.
EnterpriseA very plausible real pattern in call duration vs. value at Axtria — longer isn't always better past some point.
Interview"How would you know you need a polynomial term instead of a plain linear fit?" — a residual plot showing a clear curved pattern (not random scatter) against a predictor is the signal.
CV-specificGood Level B conceptual knowledge — shows you know linear models can still capture real nonlinearity through feature engineering.

Ridge, Lasso & Elastic Net — Full Math A

Ridge adds λΣβᵢ² to the OLS loss; Lasso adds λΣ|βᵢ|; Elastic Net combines both penalties. λ (alpha in sklearn) controls penalty strength — λ=0 recovers plain OLS, very large λ shrinks all coefficients toward (Ridge) or to (Lasso) zero.

Worked example
from sklearn.linear_model import ElasticNet from sklearn.model_selection import GridSearchCV # Elastic Net: l1_ratio balances Lasso (1.0) vs Ridge (0.0) penalty en = ElasticNet(alpha=0.5, l1_ratio=0.5) en.fit(X_train, y_train) # Tune alpha via cross-validation instead of guessing grid = GridSearchCV(ElasticNet(), {"alpha":[0.01,0.1,1,10], "l1_ratio":[0.1,0.5,0.9]}, cv=5) grid.fit(X_train, y_train) print(grid.best_params_) # {'alpha': 0.1, 'l1_ratio': 0.5}
Elastic Net's l1_ratio=0.5 gives Lasso's feature-selection ability (some coefficients can hit exactly zero) combined with Ridge's stability when predictors are correlated — a genuine best-of-both-worlds compromise, chosen here via cross-validation rather than a fixed guess.
Daily-lifePacking a suitcase: Ridge trims everything a little, Lasso leaves some items home entirely, Elastic Net does a bit of both.
ConsumerA recommendation model with hundreds of weakly-correlated features benefits from Elastic Net's balance over pure Lasso or Ridge alone.
BusinessA pricing model with many correlated cost-driver features (where pure Lasso might arbitrarily drop one of two correlated features) benefits from Elastic Net's more stable behavior.
EnterpriseDirectly relevant if Axtria's models needed to manage a feature set with some multicollinearity (Stage 2) — a defensible thing to say you'd reach for.
Interview"Why would you choose Elastic Net over plain Lasso?" — when predictors are correlated, Lasso can arbitrarily pick one and zero the other; Elastic Net's Ridge component tends to keep correlated groups together.
CV-specificA genuinely sophisticated answer beyond the Stage 6 introduction — full math and a real reason to prefer the hybrid.
Alternative Models to Linear Regression — Why This, Why Not That

Whenever Linear Regression is a CV pointer, the honest, interview-ready answer covers the realistic alternative set, not just the one model used:

Decision Tree / Random Forest Regressor — captures nonlinearity and interactions automatically without manual feature engineering, at the cost of interpretability (Stage 8). KNN Regressor — simple, no training phase, but slow at prediction time and suffers from the curse of dimensionality (Stage 6). Polynomial/Ridge/Lasso Regression — stay within the linear-model family, keeping interpretability while adding flexibility or stability. Why Linear Regression was a reasonable choice regardless: when stakeholders need to see and trust exactly how each input drives the prediction, and the relationship is roughly linear, the interpretability trade is usually worth it over a small accuracy gain from a black-box alternative.

Logistic Regression

Full standalone depth — the model genuinely used at Axtria. Sigmoid, log-odds, MLE, regularization, threshold traps, multiclass, and the alternative-model set.

Why Not Linear Regression for Classification A

Linear Regression can output any value from -∞ to +∞ — nonsensical for a probability, which must stay in [0,1]. It also assumes constant, additive effects and normally-distributed errors, both violated by a binary 0/1 target.

Worked example
# Fitting plain Linear Regression to a 0/1 "converted" target: model = LinearRegression().fit(X, y_binary) preds = model.predict(X_new) print(preds) # [-0.14, 1.32, 0.61, ...] — negative and >1 values, meaningless as probabilities
A predicted "probability" of -0.14 or 1.32 is literally nonsensical — this single concrete failure is the cleanest way to demonstrate why classification needs a different model, not just a "we usually don't do it that way" hand-wave.
Daily-lifeAnswering "will it rain?" with "-14% chance" makes no sense — probabilities have to live between 0% and 100%.
ConsumerA spam-probability score can't sensibly be reported as -30% or 150%.
BusinessA conversion-probability model output feeding a dashboard needs to be a clean, boundable percentage stakeholders can trust.
EnterpriseThe exact, concrete reason Logistic Regression (not Linear Regression) was the right choice for Axtria's high-value-call classification.
Interview"Why not just threshold a linear regression's output at 0.5?" — you still get out-of-range predictions, and the loss function (squared error) isn't well-suited to a binary target's actual distribution.
Your CVThe clean, confident opening answer for "why Logistic Regression" in any Axtria-related interview question.

The Sigmoid Function & Log-Odds (Logit) A

Logistic Regression models the log-odds (logit) as a linear function of the predictors: log(p/(1-p)) = β₀ + β₁x₁ + ... The sigmoid function σ(z) = 1/(1+e⁻ᶻ) then maps that unbounded linear score back into a valid [0,1] probability.

Worked example
import math # Linear score (logit): z = -1.2 + 0.05*duration + 0.3*tenure z = -1.2 + 0.05*20 + 0.3*3 # duration=20min, tenure=3yr print(z) # 0.7 p = 1 / (1 + math.exp(-z)) print(round(p, 3)) # 0.668 — a valid probability, guaranteed in [0,1] no matter what z is
No matter how extreme z gets (say z=50 or z=-50), the sigmoid squashes it into (0,1) — this is precisely the mechanism that fixes the out-of-range problem plain Linear Regression had.
Daily-lifeA dimmer switch that maps any input voltage smoothly into a 0-100% brightness range — no matter how hard you push it, the output stays bounded.
ConsumerAny "confidence score" a recommendation or fraud system shows you (e.g. "87% match") is typically a sigmoid-squashed linear score under the hood.
BusinessConverting a raw "propensity score" into a clean, presentable conversion probability for a sales dashboard.
EnterpriseThe literal mechanism inside Axtria's Logistic Regression model turning call/rep features into a bounded conversion probability.
Interview"Derive the sigmoid from the log-odds equation." — solve log(p/(1-p))=z for p: p = 1/(1+e⁻ᶻ) — a common whiteboard question.
CV-specificThe single most important piece of math to have cold for this model — practice deriving it without notes.

Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) B

Logistic Regression's coefficients are fit by finding the values that maximize the likelihood of observing the actual training labels — not by minimizing squared error like OLS. The loss function used is log-loss (cross-entropy), which MLE naturally leads to for a Bernoulli-distributed target.

Worked example
# Log-loss for one example: -[y*log(p) + (1-y)*log(1-p)] # If actual label y=1 and model predicted p=0.9 (confident, correct): loss_good = -(1*math.log(0.9) + 0*math.log(0.1)) print(round(loss_good, 3)) # 0.105 — small loss # If actual label y=1 but model predicted p=0.1 (confident, WRONG): loss_bad = -(1*math.log(0.1) + 0*math.log(0.9)) print(round(loss_bad, 3)) # 2.303 — much larger loss
Log-loss penalizes confident-and-wrong predictions far more severely than uncertain ones — a model predicting p=0.1 when the true label is 1 pays over 20x the loss of a correct, confident prediction, which is exactly the behavior you want from a well-calibrated probability model.
Daily-lifeBeing loudly, confidently wrong about something (e.g. insisting a false fact is true) is judged far more harshly than being unsure and wrong.
ConsumerA weather app that's 95% confident of sun on a rainy day should be penalized more than one that said 55%.
BusinessA conversion model that's overconfident on calls that don't convert erodes stakeholder trust faster than one with well-calibrated uncertainty.
EnterpriseLevel B honest framing: understanding that MLE/log-loss, not squared error, is what's actually optimized under the hood when sklearn fits Logistic Regression.
Interview"Why not just use squared error loss for classification?" — squared error doesn't penalize confident wrong predictions as sharply and can lead to slower, less well-behaved optimization for a probability target.
CV-specificA depth signal that separates candidates who've only called .fit() from those who understand what's being optimized.

Interpreting Coefficients as Odds Ratios A

Because the model is linear in log-odds, exponentiating a coefficient (e^β) gives the odds ratio — how much the odds of the positive outcome multiply for a one-unit increase in that predictor, holding others constant.

Worked example
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression import numpy as np model = LogisticRegression().fit(X_train, y_train) odds_ratios = np.exp(model.coef_[0]) print(dict(zip(X_train.columns, odds_ratios))) # {'duration_min': 1.04, 'rep_tenure': 1.35, 'prior_orders': 0.88}
rep_tenure's odds ratio of 1.35 means each additional year of tenure multiplies the odds of conversion by 1.35 (a 35% increase in odds, holding other features fixed); prior_orders' 0.88 means each additional prior order slightly decreases conversion odds (12% decrease) — direct, stakeholder-presentable statements.
Daily-life"Each cup of coffee multiplies your odds of staying awake past midnight by 1.4x" — a natural, odds-based way people already reason about risk.
ConsumerA credit-scoring model reporting "each missed payment multiplies default odds by 2.1x" in a plain-language explanation.
BusinessPresenting to a VP: "each additional year of rep tenure is associated with 35% higher odds of a call converting."
EnterpriseExactly the kind of stakeholder-facing statement your CVs' "stakeholder-facing delivery" claims can point to concretely.
Interview"What's the difference between an odds ratio and a probability?" — odds = p/(1-p); an odds ratio describes a multiplicative effect on odds, not a direct percentage-point change in probability, which is a common interview trap.
Your CVOne of the strongest, most quotable Level A pieces of knowledge on this entire page — practice saying it fluently.

Decision Threshold & Threshold-Tuning Traps A

Logistic Regression outputs a probability; converting it to a 0/1 decision requires a threshold, defaulting to 0.5 — which is rarely the right business choice, especially under class imbalance (Stage 6).

Worked example
from sklearn.metrics import precision_recall_curve probs = model.predict_proba(X_test)[:,1] precisions, recalls, thresholds = precision_recall_curve(y_test, probs) # At threshold=0.5: precision=0.55, recall=0.30 (misses most positives) # At threshold=0.25: precision=0.38, recall=0.71 (catches far more, # at the cost of more false positives — a real business tradeoff)
Simply lowering the threshold from 0.5 to 0.25 more than doubled recall (0.30 → 0.71) at the cost of lower precision — the "right" threshold depends entirely on whether missing a real opportunity or wasting rep time on a false flag is more costly to the business.
Daily-lifeA smoke detector's sensitivity dial — too sensitive means false alarms, not sensitive enough means missed real fires; 0.5 isn't automatically the right setting.
ConsumerA fraud system might use a much lower threshold (flag more, err toward caution) than a spam filter (higher threshold, avoid burying real email).
BusinessA limited-capacity sales team might need a higher threshold (only the most confident leads) than an unlimited-outreach email campaign.
EnterpriseA very plausible, honest thing to say was considered at Axtria — the threshold likely wasn't left at the sklearn default given the business's specific rep-capacity constraints.
Interview"Is 0.5 always the right threshold?" — no, virtually never by default; it should be tuned against the actual relative cost of false positives vs. false negatives for the specific business decision.
CV-specificA sophisticated, business-grounded answer that directly builds on Stage 6's precision/recall material.

Regularization in Logistic Regression A

Just like Linear Regression, Logistic Regression can overfit with many features or a small sample size — L1/L2 penalties apply the same way, added to the log-loss objective instead of squared error.

Worked example
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression # sklearn's C parameter is INVERSE regularization strength # (smaller C = stronger regularization — easy interview trap) strong_reg = LogisticRegression(penalty="l2", C=0.01).fit(X_train, y_train) weak_reg = LogisticRegression(penalty="l2", C=100).fit(X_train, y_train) print(strong_reg.coef_) # smaller-magnitude coefficients print(weak_reg.coef_) # larger-magnitude coefficients, closer to unregularized MLE
This is a genuinely common interview trap: sklearn's C is the inverse of regularization strength — smaller C means stronger regularization, the opposite of what most people assume from the variable name.
Daily-lifeSame "trim the suitcase" idea as Ridge/Lasso for linear regression — the mechanism transfers directly.
ConsumerA high-dimensional text-classification model (thousands of word features) relies heavily on regularization to avoid overfitting to rare words.
BusinessA conversion model with many candidate features (call metadata, rep history, timing) benefits from regularization to avoid overfitting to noise.
EnterprisePlausible, honest thing to describe understanding at Axtria without necessarily having personally tuned the exact C value.
Interview"What does a smaller C value do in sklearn's LogisticRegression?" — the classic trap: smaller C = MORE regularization, not less.
CV-specificKnowing this specific sklearn gotcha cold is a small, high-value signal of real hands-on familiarity.

Multiclass Logistic Regression: One-vs-Rest & Multinomial B

One-vs-Rest (OvR): trains one binary classifier per class (class vs. all others), picks the class with the highest predicted probability. Multinomial (Softmax): a single unified model that directly models all classes' probabilities jointly, generalizing the sigmoid to multiple classes.

Worked example
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression # 3-class problem: call outcome = {no_answer, no_sale, sale} model = LogisticRegression(multi_class="multinomial", max_iter=1000).fit(X_train, y_train) probs = model.predict_proba(X_test) print(probs[0]) # [0.15, 0.25, 0.60] — sums to exactly 1.0 across all 3 classes
Multinomial's softmax output sums to exactly 1.0 across all classes by construction — OvR's independently-trained binary probabilities are NOT guaranteed to sum to 1, a subtle but real difference between the two approaches.
Daily-lifeOvR is like separately asking "is it a cat? is it a dog? is it a bird?" three times; multinomial is asking "which of these three is it?" once, jointly.
ConsumerA support-ticket router classifying into "billing," "technical," "sales" categories at once.
BusinessClassifying calls into more than two outcomes (e.g. no_answer / no_sale / sale) rather than a simple binary conversion flag.
EnterpriseA natural, honest extension to describe if Axtria's classification work ever needed more than two outcome categories.
Interview"When would you prefer OvR over multinomial?" — OvR is simpler and parallelizable; multinomial is generally more statistically principled when classes are mutually exclusive, which is the more common real-world case.
CV-specificGood Level B depth showing the binary Logistic Regression story generalizes cleanly.
Alternative Models to Logistic Regression — Why This, Why Not That

Decision Tree Classifier — captures nonlinear decision boundaries and interactions automatically, more interpretable to a non-technical audience via visual rules, but less stable and prone to overfitting unconstrained (Stage 8). Naive Bayes — very fast, works well with high-dimensional sparse data (e.g. text), but its strong feature-independence assumption is usually unrealistic for correlated business features like call duration and rep tenure. KNN — simple, no explicit training, but slow at prediction time on large datasets and sensitive to feature scaling/dimensionality. SVM — can be very accurate with the right kernel, but less interpretable and slower to train on large data than Logistic Regression. Why Logistic Regression was the right call at Axtria regardless: probabilistic output the business could act on directly, coefficients that map to odds ratios stakeholders can sanity-check, and fast enough to retrain regularly — the accuracy ceiling of alternatives rarely justified the loss of interpretability for this specific use case.

Red Flags — Never Say This

"Logistic Regression is called regression because it predicts a continuous number." Wrong — it predicts a probability used for classification; the name refers to the underlying log-odds linear model, not a continuous output.
"A smaller C value in sklearn means weaker regularization." Backwards — C is the inverse of regularization strength; smaller C means stronger regularization.
"An odds ratio of 1.35 means a 35 percentage-point increase in probability." Wrong — it's a 35% multiplicative increase in odds, not a direct percentage-point change in probability; conflating the two is a common, exposable error.
"0.5 is the correct classification threshold by default." Rarely true — it should be chosen based on the actual relative business cost of false positives vs. false negatives.
"Lasso and Ridge always give the same coefficients, just scaled differently." False — only Lasso can force coefficients to exactly zero; this is a structural difference, not just a matter of scale.
