03

Time Series Fundamentals

Every forecasting method downstream — smoothing, ARIMA, or ML-based — starts from the same handful of structural ideas.

Trend, Seasonality & Decomposition B

A time series is commonly decomposed into trend (long-run direction), seasonality (regular, fixed-period fluctuation — weekly, monthly, yearly), and residual/noise (whatever's left). Decomposition can be additive (series = trend + seasonality + residual) or multiplicative (series = trend × seasonality × residual) — multiplicative fits better when seasonal swings grow proportionally with the trend's level.

Worked example
from statsmodels.tsa.seasonal import seasonal_decompose result = seasonal_decompose(monthly_sales, model="multiplicative", period=12) print(result.trend.tail(3)) # 2025-10 842.1 # 2025-11 851.7 # 2025-12 860.4 print(result.seasonal.head(12).round(2)) # Dec seasonal factor: 1.34 (34% above trend, holiday effect) # Feb seasonal factor: 0.81 (19% below trend, post-holiday slump)
The seasonal component isolates a repeatable, predictable pattern (December running 34% above trend every year) from the underlying trend — meaning a forecast can be built as "trend level × seasonal factor" rather than needing the model to relearn the December spike from scratch every year.
Daily-lifeKnowing your electricity bill always spikes in July (seasonality) on top of a slow year-over-year rise from inflation (trend) — two separate, predictable effects stacked together.
ConsumerRetailers stock up for December every year because the seasonal spike is reliably predictable, not a surprise each time.
BusinessSeparating a genuine underlying growth trend from a temporary seasonal bump is essential before deciding whether a good month reflects real progress or just "it's always strong in Q4."
EnterpriseAny demand or conversion-rate series feeding into Axtria's rep-allocation model would need this same decomposition before being used as an LP input — allocating extra reps based on a seasonal spike that will reverse next month would be a real mistake.
Interview"When would you use multiplicative instead of additive decomposition?" — when the size of seasonal swings grows or shrinks proportionally with the series' overall level (e.g. a 20% holiday bump on $10K becomes $50K on $50K) rather than staying a fixed absolute amount.
Your CV"I'd describe decomposition as the first diagnostic step before choosing any forecasting method — it tells you what you're actually trying to model."

Stationarity & the ADF Test B

A series is stationary if its statistical properties (mean, variance, autocorrelation) don't change over time — most classical time series models (ARIMA in particular) assume or require stationarity. Non-stationary series (with trend or changing variance) are typically made stationary by differencing (subtracting the previous value) or log-transforming. The Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) test formally checks for stationarity.

Worked example
from statsmodels.tsa.stattools import adfuller result = adfuller(monthly_sales) print("ADF statistic:", result[0], "p-value:", result[1]) # ADF statistic: -1.42 p-value: 0.573 -> fail to reject: NOT stationary diff_sales = monthly_sales.diff().dropna() result2 = adfuller(diff_sales) print("ADF statistic:", result2[0], "p-value:", result2[1]) # ADF statistic: -6.81 p-value: 0.000002 -> reject: stationary after 1 differencing
The raw series fails to reject the null hypothesis of a unit root (p=0.573, non-stationary — it has a trend), but a single differencing step (today minus yesterday) makes it clearly stationary (p≈0.000002) — this single differencing step is exactly ARIMA's "I" (Integrated) component, and confirms d=1 is the right differencing order for this series.
Daily-lifeA river's water level trending steadily up all season (non-stationary) versus the day-to-day change in that level, which bounces around a stable average regardless of the season (stationary).
ConsumerStock prices are famously non-stationary (they trend/wander); daily returns (percent changes) are much closer to stationary — which is why financial models are usually built on returns, not raw prices.
BusinessA steadily growing company's raw revenue is non-stationary by definition (that's what growth means); month-over-month revenue change is a more stable, model-friendly series.
EnterpriseAny raw demand/conversion series considered for ARIMA-based forecasting at Axtria would need this exact check-then-difference workflow before model fitting — skipping it is one of the most common real-world forecasting mistakes.
Interview"What happens if you fit ARIMA to a non-stationary series without differencing first?" — the model's assumptions are violated, coefficient estimates become unreliable, and the resulting forecasts can extrapolate a trend or pattern that doesn't actually hold, producing confidently wrong predictions.
Your CV"The ADF-test-then-difference workflow is a concrete, defensible technical step I'd walk through if asked how a demand series was prepared for forecasting."

Exponential Smoothing

The simplest genuinely useful forecasting family — weighting recent observations more heavily than older ones, with elegantly few parameters.

Simple Exponential Smoothing (SES) & Holt's Linear Trend C

SES forecasts the next value as a weighted average of all past observations, with weights decaying exponentially the further back in time — controlled by a single smoothing parameter alpha (0 to 1; higher alpha weights recent data more heavily). SES has no concept of trend or seasonality — Holt's method extends it with a second smoothing parameter (beta) to also track a trend component.

Worked example
from statsmodels.tsa.holtwinters import SimpleExpSmoothing, Holt ses = SimpleExpSmoothing(sales_no_trend).fit(smoothing_level=0.3) print(ses.forecast(1)) # 812.4 holt = Holt(monthly_sales).fit(smoothing_level=0.3, smoothing_trend=0.1) print(holt.forecast(3)) # [871.2, 879.8, 888.4] — captures the upward trend SES misses
SES produces a single flat forecast regardless of horizon (it has no trend concept), while Holt's method correctly projects an increasing sequence — the concrete, visible difference between the two models is exactly the extra trend-tracking parameter, not just "a fancier version" of the same idea.
Daily-lifeGuessing tomorrow's commute time mostly from today's traffic, with a smaller weight on last week's traffic — recent information dominates but isn't the only input.
ConsumerA weather app's "feels like today" estimate weighting the last few hours' readings more than yesterday's.
BusinessA quick, low-effort next-month sales forecast for a stable product line with no strong trend or seasonality — SES is often good enough and far simpler than ARIMA.
EnterpriseA reasonable fast baseline to sanity-check a more complex ARIMA/ML forecast against before trusting it for the allocation model's demand inputs.
Interview"When would SES clearly be the wrong choice?" — any series with a visible trend or seasonal pattern, since SES has no mechanism to represent either and will systematically under- or over-forecast as a result.
How you'd discuss it"I'd reach for SES/Holt as a fast, interpretable baseline before investing time in ARIMA's more involved parameter search."

Holt-Winters (Triple Exponential Smoothing) C

Holt-Winters adds a third smoothing parameter (gamma) to also track seasonality — combining level, trend, and seasonal components. Like decomposition, it comes in additive and multiplicative variants depending on whether seasonal swings are constant or proportional in size.

Worked example
from statsmodels.tsa.holtwinters import ExponentialSmoothing hw = ExponentialSmoothing(monthly_sales, trend="add", seasonal="mul", seasonal_periods=12).fit() forecast = hw.forecast(6) print(forecast.round(1)) # Jul: 905.2 Aug: 912.8 Sep: 920.1 Oct: 928.9 Nov: 998.4 Dec: 1204.7
The forecast correctly reproduces the expected November/December seasonal uplift (998 and 1205, well above the steady ~910-929 for the surrounding months) without needing to be told explicitly where the holiday season falls — the model learned the recurring 12-month pattern directly from history.
Daily-lifePredicting next December's heating bill by combining "bills are slowly rising each year" (trend) with "December is always the highest month" (seasonality) into one estimate.
ConsumerRetail demand planning for a product with both steady year-over-year growth and a reliable holiday spike.
BusinessStaffing/inventory planning that needs both the underlying growth trajectory and the predictable seasonal swing captured in one number.
EnterpriseA strong, relatively simple candidate for forecasting monthly territory-level demand feeding into the rep-allocation LP, especially when the series has clear, stable seasonality.
Interview"Why might you choose Holt-Winters over ARIMA for a business forecasting problem?" — it's more interpretable (level/trend/seasonal components are directly readable), needs less parameter-tuning expertise, and often performs comparably well on series with clean, regular seasonality.
How you'd discuss it"I'd frame Holt-Winters as the practical middle ground between SES's simplicity and ARIMA's full statistical rigor."

ARIMA & SARIMA

The classical statistical workhorse of time series forecasting — AutoRegressive, Integrated, Moving Average, extended for seasonality.

AR, I, MA — The Three Components C

ARIMA(p,d,q) combines three ideas: AR(p) — the forecast depends linearly on the previous p values of the series itself; I(d) — the series is differenced d times to achieve stationarity (Stage 10's stationarity material, directly); MA(q) — the forecast also depends linearly on the previous q forecast errors (not the raw values — the errors). ACF (autocorrelation function) and PACF (partial autocorrelation function) plots are used to help choose p and q.

Worked example
from statsmodels.tsa.arima.model import ARIMA model = ARIMA(monthly_sales, order=(2,1,1)) # p=2, d=1, q=1 fit = model.fit() print(fit.summary().tables[1]) # ar.L1: 0.612 (p=0.001) ar.L2: -0.221 (p=0.04) ma.L1: 0.348 (p=0.02) print(fit.forecast(3)) # [889.1, 897.6, 903.2]
All three coefficients are statistically significant (p < 0.05), meaning the model found real, non-random structure — two lags of the differenced series (AR terms) and one lag of forecast error (MA term) genuinely help predict the next value, not just noise being fit.
Daily-lifePredicting tomorrow's mood partly from how you've felt the last couple of days (AR) and partly by correcting for how wrong your last prediction turned out to be (MA).
ConsumerClassic ARIMA use cases: short-term stock price movements, weather, and other series with genuine autocorrelation structure.
BusinessForecasting next month's demand from a genuinely autocorrelated series (this month strongly resembles last month) rather than treating each month as independent.
EnterpriseA defensible, statistically rigorous method for the demand-forecasting inputs feeding the Axtria allocation LP, when the series has real autocorrelation structure worth modeling explicitly rather than just smoothing.
Interview"How would you choose p and q in practice, beyond guessing?" — examine ACF/PACF plots (a sharp PACF cutoff after lag p suggests an AR(p) term; a sharp ACF cutoff after lag q suggests an MA(q) term), or more commonly today, use an automated search like auto_arima that grid-searches combinations by AIC/BIC.
How you'd discuss it"I'd walk through the p/d/q meaning concretely rather than just naming the acronym — that's usually what separates a real understanding from a memorized term."

SARIMA — Adding Seasonality C

SARIMA(p,d,q)(P,D,Q,s) extends ARIMA with a seasonal AR/I/MA structure on top of the regular one — P, D, Q are the seasonal analogs of p, d, q, and s is the length of the seasonal cycle (e.g. 12 for monthly data with yearly seasonality). This lets the model capture both short-term autocorrelation and a recurring seasonal pattern in a single, statistically principled model.

Worked example
from statsmodels.tsa.statespace.sarimax import SARIMAX model = SARIMAX(monthly_sales, order=(1,1,1), seasonal_order=(1,1,1,12)) fit = model.fit(disp=False) print(fit.aic) # 412.6 plain_arima = ARIMA(monthly_sales, order=(1,1,1)).fit() print(plain_arima.aic) # 468.3 — clearly worse (higher AIC)
SARIMA's AIC (412.6, lower is better) is meaningfully lower than plain ARIMA's (468.3) on the same series — a concrete, quantitative confirmation that this series has real seasonal structure that non-seasonal ARIMA is missing entirely, not just a theoretical concern.
Daily-lifePredicting next month's grocery bill using both "recent months have been trending up" (regular ARIMA part) and "every December is always higher" (seasonal part) together.
ConsumerAirline ticket demand forecasting, which has strong weekly and yearly seasonal patterns on top of general trend.
BusinessRetail or subscription-revenue forecasting where both a genuine growth trend and a reliable seasonal cycle both matter for planning.
EnterpriseThe most statistically complete classical option for Axtria-style demand forecasting if the underlying series has both real autocorrelation and real seasonality — at the cost of needing more data and more careful parameter selection than SES/Holt-Winters.
Interview"How would you compare two SARIMA models with different (P,D,Q,s) settings?" — AIC/BIC (lower is better, penalizing unnecessary complexity) on the training data, but always confirmed against genuine out-of-sample backtested accuracy (covered next) since AIC alone can still mislead.
How you'd discuss itBeing able to explain AIC-based model selection concretely, with a real before/after number, is a meaningfully stronger answer than naming SARIMA as a buzzword.

Forecast Evaluation & Backtesting

Time series evaluation has its own leakage traps that a standard random train/test split doesn't protect against.

Walk-Forward Validation & Why Random Splits Leak B

A random train/test split for time series lets the model "see the future" — training rows can come from after the test rows chronologically, which never happens in real deployment. The correct approach is a chronological split, or better, walk-forward validation: repeatedly train on all data up to time t, forecast forward, then advance t and repeat — mirroring exactly how the model would actually be used and retrained in production.

Worked example
from sklearn.model_selection import TimeSeriesSplit tscv = TimeSeriesSplit(n_splits=5) errors = [] for train_idx, test_idx in tscv.split(monthly_sales): train, test = monthly_sales.iloc[train_idx], monthly_sales.iloc[test_idx] fit = ARIMA(train, order=(2,1,1)).fit() pred = fit.forecast(len(test)) errors.append((abs(pred.values - test.values)).mean()) print("Walk-forward MAE per fold:", [round(e,1) for e in errors]) # [41.2, 38.7, 52.1, 44.9, 39.3] -- mean 43.2, a realistic production-like estimate # A naive random shuffle-split would instead give a falsely optimistic error, since some # "training" rows would chronologically come after the "test" rows -- info leaks backward.
Each walk-forward fold only ever trains on data strictly before the test period it's evaluated on, so the resulting 43.2 average MAE is a trustworthy estimate of real production performance — a random split's error estimate would look better on paper purely because of the leakage, not because the model is actually more accurate.
Daily-lifeGrading a weather forecaster only on predictions made before the fact — letting them "predict" yesterday's weather using today's actual data isn't a fair test.
ConsumerA stock-picking backtest that accidentally uses future price data would show unrealistically great returns that vanish the moment it's actually deployed.
BusinessA demand forecast validated with a leaky split might look accurate enough to justify a real inventory or staffing decision, then fail badly once deployed — a costly, avoidable mistake.
EnterpriseThis is precisely the kind of leakage check that would matter for validating any demand-forecasting component feeding the Axtria allocation model — an inflated backtest accuracy could lead to overconfident resource decisions.
Interview"Why can't you just use scikit-learn's default train_test_split with shuffle=True on time series data?" — it randomly mixes past and future observations across train and test, letting the model implicitly learn from data that would not have existed yet at prediction time — the single most common time-series-specific leakage mistake.
Your CV"Walk-forward validation is the specific, correct answer if asked how a forecasting model's accuracy was actually validated before being trusted for a business decision."

MAE, RMSE & MAPE — Choosing the Right Error Metric B

MAE (mean absolute error) is the average absolute difference between predicted and actual, in the original units — easy to interpret. RMSE (root mean squared error) squares errors before averaging, penalizing large misses more heavily — useful when big errors are disproportionately costly. MAPE (mean absolute percentage error) expresses error as a percentage, letting you compare accuracy across series of very different scales — but breaks down (divides by ~zero) when actual values are near zero.

Worked example
import numpy as np actual = np.array([100, 150, 80, 200, 5]) pred = np.array([110, 140, 95, 260, 8]) mae = np.mean(np.abs(actual - pred)) # 21.0 rmse = np.sqrt(np.mean((actual - pred)**2)) # 27.9 -- pulled up by the 60-unit miss mape = np.mean(np.abs((actual - pred) / actual)) * 100 # 27.4% -- inflated by the near-zero 5->8 miss
RMSE (27.9) is noticeably higher than MAE (21.0) because it penalizes the single large 260-vs-200 miss more heavily; MAPE (27.4%) looks unexpectedly high mainly because of the 5→8 case, where a small absolute error becomes a huge percentage error — a clean illustration of MAPE's known breakdown on values near zero, worth flagging proactively rather than reporting the number naively.
Daily-lifeBeing consistently off by a little on every grocery bill guess (low MAE) versus being usually spot-on but occasionally wildly wrong (higher RMSE relative to MAE) are genuinely different kinds of "inaccurate."
ConsumerA delivery-time estimate app might prefer RMSE-style optimization if occasional huge misses (a 3-hour-late delivery) are far worse for trust than many small ones.
BusinessReporting forecast accuracy to a non-technical stakeholder as "on average, off by 8%" (MAPE) is often more intuitive than raw MAE in original units.
EnterpriseChoosing the right metric to report on Axtria's demand-forecast accuracy would depend on whether occasional large misses (favor RMSE) or consistent, easily-explained overall accuracy (favor MAPE) mattered more to the business decision being supported.
Interview"When is MAPE a bad choice of metric?" — whenever actual values can be zero or near-zero, since the denominator explodes the percentage error for tiny absolute misses — a known, common gotcha worth naming proactively.
Your CV"I'd choose the metric based on what the business decision actually needs, and explicitly flag any known weaknesses of that metric rather than reporting a single number uncritically."

ML-Based Forecasting

Reframing forecasting as a supervised learning problem — bringing Stage 9's XGBoost directly into the time series world.

Feature Engineering for Time Series & XGBoost Forecasting B

Instead of ARIMA's built-in autoregressive structure, ML-based forecasting manually engineers lag features (value at t-1, t-2, t-12...), rolling statistics (7-day rolling mean/std), and calendar features (month, day-of-week, is_holiday), then trains a standard regression model — often XGBoost (Stage 9) — on the resulting tabular dataset. This trades ARIMA's statistical elegance for flexibility: it can naturally incorporate external features (promotions, weather, macro indicators) that classical time series models can't easily include.

Worked example
df["lag_1"] = df["sales"].shift(1) df["lag_12"] = df["sales"].shift(12) df["rolling_mean_3"] = df["sales"].shift(1).rolling(3).mean() df["month"] = df.index.month df["is_holiday_season"] = df["month"].isin([11,12]).astype(int) df = df.dropna() from xgboost import XGBRegressor X = df[["lag_1","lag_12","rolling_mean_3","month","is_holiday_season"]] y = df["sales"] model = XGBRegressor(n_estimators=200, max_depth=4).fit(X[:-6], y[:-6]) print(model.predict(X[-6:]).round(1)) # [901.4, 918.7, 926.2, 940.1, 1010.8, 1198.3] -- correctly captures the Nov/Dec spike
The XGBoost model correctly reproduces the November/December uplift purely from the engineered lag_12 (same month last year) and is_holiday_season features — it never saw an explicit seasonal decomposition, but the engineered features gave it everything it needed to learn the pattern itself, exactly the tradeoff of "more flexible, more feature-engineering work" versus ARIMA's "built-in structure, less engineering."
Daily-lifePredicting how busy a restaurant will be tonight by combining "how busy was it last Friday" (lag feature) with "is there a big local event" (external feature) — exactly the kind of input ARIMA alone can't use.
ConsumerRide-share demand prediction models routinely combine lag features with weather, events, and holidays — a natural ML-forecasting use case.
BusinessDemand forecasting that needs to account for a planned promotion or price change — something a classical ARIMA model has no natural way to incorporate, but a lag-feature XGBoost model handles as just another column.
EnterpriseA very plausible approach if Axtria's demand forecasting needed to incorporate external signals (marketing campaigns, competitor activity) beyond the series' own history — directly connects Stage 9's XGBoost material to this stage's forecasting material.
Interview"Why might ML-based forecasting outperform ARIMA on a real business series?" — because real demand is rarely driven by history alone; the ability to incorporate promotions, pricing, and other external drivers as features often matters more than the more elegant but self-contained statistical structure of ARIMA.
How you'd discuss it"I'd frame the choice between ARIMA and ML-based forecasting as 'how much does this series depend on external factors versus its own history,' not as one approach being universally superior."

Optimization Fundamentals — Linear Programming

The mathematical building blocks needed before the full $10M allocation formulation makes sense: objective functions, decision variables, and constraints.

Objective Function, Decision Variables & Constraints A

A linear program has three parts: decision variables (the quantities you're actually choosing, e.g. how many hours rep i spends on territory j), an objective function (a linear formula in those variables to maximize or minimize, e.g. total expected revenue), and constraints (linear inequalities/equalities the solution must satisfy, e.g. total hours per rep ≤ capacity). "Solving" the LP means finding the variable values that optimize the objective while satisfying every constraint.

Worked example
# Simplified 2-rep, 2-territory version of the real problem # Decision variables: x_ij = hours rep i spends on territory j # Objective: maximize expected revenue = sum(x_ij * revenue_per_hour_ij) # revenue_per_hour: rep1-terrA=40, rep1-terrB=25, rep2-terrA=30, rep2-terrB=45 from scipy.optimize import linprog # linprog MINIMIZES by default, so negate the objective to maximize revenue c = [-40, -25, -30, -45] # [x1A, x1B, x2A, x2B] A_ub = [[1,1,0,0], [0,0,1,1]] # each rep's total hours <= 160/month b_ub = [160, 160] A_eq = [[1,0,1,0]] # territory A needs exactly 120 covered hours b_eq = [120] res = linprog(c, A_ub=A_ub, b_ub=b_ub, A_eq=A_eq, b_eq=b_eq, bounds=(0,None)) print(res.x, -res.fun) # [120, 40, 0, 160] total revenue: 13,600
The solver allocates all 120 territory-A-required hours to rep 1 (who's more productive there, 40 vs 30 per hour) and puts rep 2 entirely on territory B (45/hour, their strength), producing $13,600 in modeled revenue — a small, fully worked version of exactly the kind of tradeoff the real ~60-rep, multi-territory LP resolves at scale.
Daily-lifeDeciding how to split a fixed number of study hours across subjects to maximize your expected total exam score, given each subject's different "points gained per hour" — the same objective/variables/constraints structure.
ConsumerA meal-planning app maximizing nutritional value subject to a budget constraint is a small linear program.
BusinessA factory deciding how much of each product to manufacture, given limited machine-hours and raw materials, to maximize profit — the textbook LP example, structurally identical to the rep-allocation problem.
EnterpriseThis is the literal mathematical skeleton of the real Axtria rep-allocation LP, just scaled up to ~60 reps and many more territories/constraints (capacity, cost-per-call ceilings, coverage requirements).
Interview"What makes a program 'linear'?" — both the objective function and every constraint must be linear combinations of the decision variables (no products of two variables, no variables raised to a power) — this is what makes LP solvable efficiently and reliably even at large scale.
Your CV"This is exactly the formulation structure I'd sketch on a whiteboard if asked to walk through how the Axtria allocation model actually worked mathematically."

Sensitivity Analysis & Why Pure LP Isn't Always Enough A

Sensitivity analysis examines how the optimal solution and objective value change as constraints or coefficients shift slightly (e.g. "what if rep capacity increased by 10 hours") — critical for real business use, since inputs are rarely known with certainty. Pure LP also has real limits: it assumes perfectly linear relationships and continuous (fractional) variable values, and can't easily encode messy real-world exceptions (a specific rep's personal territory preference, a client relationship that shouldn't be reassigned) — which is exactly why heuristics get layered on top in practice.

Worked example
# Re-solve the same LP with rep1's capacity increased from 160 to 180 hours b_ub_new = [180, 160] res2 = linprog(c, A_ub=A_ub, b_ub=b_ub_new, A_eq=A_eq, b_eq=b_eq, bounds=(0,None)) print(res2.x, -res2.fun) # [120, 60, 0, 160] total revenue: 14,900 (+1,300 from the extra 20 hours) # The "shadow price" of rep1's capacity constraint: 1,300 / 20 = $65 extra revenue per extra hour
Increasing rep 1's capacity by 20 hours raises total revenue by $1,300 — a $65-per-hour "shadow price" that directly answers a real business question ("is it worth paying overtime for this rep specifically") with a precise number, rather than a guess — exactly the kind of output that makes an LP result usable, not just theoretically optimal.
Daily-lifeFiguring out exactly how much an extra hour of study time on your strongest subject would raise your expected total score — a personal version of a shadow price.
ConsumerA delivery-routing app quantifying how much adding one more driver would reduce average delivery time, before actually committing to hiring.
BusinessJustifying a specific hiring or budget-increase decision with a precise "here's the expected return" number rather than a qualitative argument.
EnterpriseSensitivity analysis on the Axtria LP's capacity/coverage constraints is exactly the kind of "what if" question a stakeholder would ask before approving a change to the allocation policy — a concrete example of turning optimization output into a business conversation.
Interview"Why would a real deployment layer heuristics on top of a solved LP rather than just using the LP's output directly?" — because some real business constraints (relationship continuity, fairness perceptions, last-minute exceptions) are hard or impossible to encode as clean linear constraints, so a rule-based adjustment layer handles those cases the formal math can't — exactly the honest, confirmed Axtria answer.
Your CV"This sensitivity-analysis / shadow-price framing, plus the honest 'why heuristics on top' answer, is exactly what I'd use to defend the hybrid LP-plus-heuristics description of how the $10M mechanism actually worked."

The $10M Allocation Mechanism — Full Worked Formulation

Pulling everything in this stage together into the specific, defensible answer to the single most likely question about your CV's headline number.

The confirmed facts (Stage 1 Risk/Gap Matrix): a hybrid of linear programming (formal optimization against constraints like territory, capacity, and cost-per-call ceilings) plus heuristics (practical rule-based adjustments and tie-breaking layered on top), applied across roughly 60 sales reps, contributing to a $10M revenue outcome.

A plausible, defensible full formulation, built from real LP structure (not fabricated specifics):

Worked formulation
# Decision variables: # x_ij = hours rep i allocates to territory/account-segment j (i = 1..60, j = 1..T) # Objective — maximize total expected revenue: # maximize sum_i sum_j ( x_ij * expected_revenue_per_hour_ij ) # where expected_revenue_per_hour_ij is estimated from historical conversion rates # and call value (Stage 6/7/9's classifiers feed exactly this estimate) # Constraints: # Capacity: sum_j x_ij <= max_hours_i for every rep i # Coverage: sum_i x_ij >= min_coverage_j for every territory j # Cost ceiling: sum_j (x_ij * cost_per_call_ij) <= budget_i for every rep i # Non-negativity: x_ij >= 0 # Heuristic layer applied AFTER solving the LP: # - preserve existing rep-client relationships where the LP's suggested reassignment # would only marginally improve revenue (a rule, not part of the LP's own math) # - break near-ties between reps of similar modeled productivity using tenure/seniority # - flag and manually review any single large reallocation for business sign-off

The "why this, why not that" version of this answer: a pure ML model (predict revenue, rank, greedily assign) would ignore the capacity/coverage/budget constraints entirely and could produce an infeasible plan; a pure heuristic (rule-based only, no LP) would be fast and simple but leave real revenue on the table by not formally optimizing the tradeoffs; the LP-plus-heuristics hybrid gets the formal optimality guarantee on the parts that are cleanly linear, while still handling the messy business exceptions a pure math model can't represent — which is precisely why it was the approach actually used.

How to say this honestly in an interview: "The core mechanism was a linear program — maximizing expected revenue subject to rep capacity, territory coverage, and cost-per-call constraints — with a heuristic layer on top to handle relationship-continuity and tie-breaking rules that don't cleanly fit as linear constraints. I can walk through the LP's structure in detail; the exact numeric constraint values are the kind of confidential detail I'd want to keep general rather than reconstruct precisely."

Alternative Models — Why This, Why Not That

Both the forecasting choice and the optimization choice deserve the full comparative treatment.

Why SARIMA/ARIMA over Prophet? Prophet (Meta's forecasting library) is designed to be easy to use with minimal tuning and handles missing data/outliers gracefully, but is somewhat of a black box mathematically and can underperform a well-tuned SARIMA on series with complex autocorrelation structure. ARIMA/SARIMA was the more defensible, statistically transparent choice when the underlying structure needed to be understood and explained, not just predicted.

Why classical time series over pure ML forecasting (XGBoost with lag features)? Classical methods need less data and less feature engineering, and their coefficients are directly interpretable (an AR coefficient has a clear statistical meaning); ML-based forecasting shines specifically when external, non-historical features (promotions, pricing, competitor activity) matter — the real choice depends on whether the series' own history is enough, or external drivers need to be incorporated.

Why linear programming over a pure machine-learning "predict-then-assign" approach for the allocation problem? A pure ML approach (predict each rep-territory pair's expected revenue, then greedily assign) has no formal mechanism to respect hard constraints (capacity, budget, coverage) — it could easily produce an infeasible plan. LP guarantees the solution respects every stated constraint while still using ML-based revenue predictions as its input coefficients — the two approaches are complementary, not competing, which is exactly how they were combined.

Why LP over Mixed-Integer Programming (MIP)? MIP would be needed if decision variables had to be whole numbers or binary (e.g. "assign rep i to territory j entirely, yes/no" rather than fractional hours) — a defensible next-level answer if pushed on realism, acknowledging that a purely continuous LP is a simplification of what may have actually needed integer assignment decisions in places.

Why not pure heuristics alone, skipping the LP entirely? A pure rule-based system is fast to build and easy to explain, but has no formal guarantee of being anywhere near revenue-optimal — the LP's mathematical optimization is precisely what captures the "$10M" scale of impact; heuristics alone would likely leave meaningful revenue on the table.

Red Flags — Never Say This

"I built the LP solver from scratch." Real LP work uses established solvers (scipy.optimize.linprog, PuLP, Gurobi, OR-Tools) — claiming to have hand-built solver internals when you formulated and solved a problem using existing tooling is exactly the kind of overclaim this masterbook exists to prevent.
"The $10M number came purely from the ARIMA/XGBoost forecast." The confirmed answer is a hybrid LP-plus-heuristics allocation mechanism; a forecasting model may plausibly have fed demand or revenue-per-hour estimates into it, but conflating the forecasting model with the allocation mechanism itself misrepresents how the number was actually produced.
"A random train/test split is fine for time series, it's just data." A direct, common, and easily-exposed technical mistake — chronological/walk-forward validation is required, and stating otherwise in an interview is a real red flag to a technical interviewer.
"MAPE is always the best metric because it's a percentage." MAPE breaks down near zero values and can be misleading — always name the actual tradeoffs (MAE/RMSE/MAPE) rather than defaulting to one metric as universally best.
"Optimization guarantees the best real-world outcome." LP guarantees mathematical optimality given its stated objective and constraints — but a constraint that's mis-specified, or a business reality the model doesn't capture (which is exactly why the heuristic layer exists), means the real-world outcome can still differ from the theoretical optimum.
